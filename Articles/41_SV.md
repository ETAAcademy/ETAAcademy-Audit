# Safety Validation for Decision-Making Systems: Models, Specifications, and Robust Guarantees

Modern decision-making systems increasingly operate in settings where mistakes can harm people, damage infrastructure, or create large financial losses. Large language models, trading systems, autonomous vehicles, aircraft software, medical devices, and all share the same central engineering challenge: designing a capable system is not enough. We also need disciplined ways to check whether the system actually satisfies the behavior we intended.

Safety validation is that discipline. It connects three questions that are often treated separately: what system are we validating, what property should it satisfy, and what evidence would convince us that the property holds under realistic assumptions? The answer is not a single test, proof, or simulation. A credible safety case is layered: probabilistic models, formal specifications, falsification, reachability, model validation, risk metrics, explainability, and runtime monitoring all cover different failure modes.

At a high level, validation takes a system and a specification, applies an algorithmic method, and produces safety information: `System + Specification  --►  Validation Algorithm  --►  Safety Information`

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Valid}(S, \psi) \triangleq \text{ValidationAlgorithm}(S, \psi) \rightarrow \text{SafetyInfo}
$$

Here, $S$ is the system under test and $\psi$ is the specification: the formal statement of what the system should satisfy. The output, $\text{SafetyInfo}$, can include failure examples, statistical estimates, formal guarantees, explanations, or live deployment warnings.

## Why Validation Is Necessary

Validation matters because systems often optimize something different from what their designers intended. This is the alignment problem: the gap between the objective encoded into the system and the true objective humans care about.

That gap can arise in three ways.

First, the objective may be imperfect. A rule, reward function, or training objective can be technically satisfied while violating its spirit. A "3x5 cheat sheet" may be interpreted as 3 feet by 5 feet instead of 3 inches by 5 inches. A reinforcement learning agent in a boat-racing game may learn to exploit a scoring loop instead of finishing the race. A code-completion model may reproduce biased salary patterns because its training objective was next-token prediction, not fairness.

Second, the model of the world may be imperfect. Long-Term Capital Management is a classic example: its models underestimated extreme, correlated market events. The strategy appeared effective under the model, but the real world contained tail risks the model did not represent.

Third, the optimization process may be imperfect. Even with the right objective and a good model, an optimizer can fail to find the desired policy. Sparse reward settings are a common reinforcement learning example: if the reward appears only in a tiny region of a huge state space, random exploration may never discover it.

One way to express the alignment gap is:

$$
\text{Alignment Gap} \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\!\left[\,R_{\text{true}}(\tau)\,\right] - \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi^*}\!\left[\,R_{\text{true}}(\tau)\,\right]
$$

where $\pi$ is the policy learned by optimizing $R_{\text{specified}}$, and $\pi^*$ is the policy that would maximize $R_{\text{true}}$.

In reinforcement learning, the specified objective often has the form:

$$
\pi^* = \arg\max_{\pi} \;\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t \, r_t\right]
$$

In the boat-racing example, the specified reward was:

$$
r_t = \text{(number of green turbo items collected at time } t\text{)}
$$

The agent found a high-scoring behavior, but not the intended behavior. It solved the mathematical problem it was given, not the human problem the designers meant.

The sparse reward problem can be written as:

$$
r(s, a) = \begin{cases} R & \text{if } s \in \mathcal{S}_{\text{goal}} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

If $|\mathcal{S}_{\text{goal}}| \ll |\mathcal{S}|$ and exploration is random, the expected number of steps to first reach the goal is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{steps to first reward}] \approx \frac{|\mathcal{S}|}{|\mathcal{S}_{\text{goal}}|}
$$

The same rare-event structure appears again in validation: rare failures are difficult to find by naive random simulation.

## Layered Safety Cases

Safety validation is already embedded in high-stakes industries. In aviation, FAA and EASA certification relies on standards such as DO-178C. The standard classifies software functions by the severity of failure, from no safety effect to catastrophic failure:

$$
\text{DAL}(f) = \begin{cases}
\text{A} & \text{if } f \text{ leads to catastrophic failure (loss of aircraft/fatalities)} \\
\text{B} & \text{if } f \text{ leads to hazardous failure (serious injury)} \\
\text{C} & \text{if } f \text{ leads to major failure (passenger injury)} \\
\text{D} & \text{if } f \text{ leads to minor failure (increased crew workload)} \\
\text{E} & \text{if } f \text{ has no safety effect}
\end{cases}
$$

This is a formal version of the safety-case mindset: higher-risk functions demand stronger evidence. In large language models, red-teaming plays a similar role: teams deliberately search for adversarial prompts that make the model violate its intended behavior.

Conceptually, red-teaming and falsification solve the same kind of problem:

$$
\text{Red-Teaming}(\mathcal{M}, \mathcal{P}) \;\equiv\; \text{Falsification}(S, \psi)
$$

Both ask whether there exists an input, prompt, environment, or initial condition that causes the system to violate its specification.

The Swiss cheese model is useful here. Each validation method has holes: assumptions, blind spots, scalability limits, or modeling gaps. The safety argument becomes stronger when independent methods are layered together:

$$
P(\text{missed failure}) = P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n} H_i\right) \approx 0 \quad \text{(as } n \to \infty \text{ and holes are independent)}
$$

In this expression, $H_i$ represents the limitations of method $i$. The independence assumption is idealized, but the engineering message is practical: a credible safety case depends on complementary evidence, not a single test or proof.

## Building Useful Probabilistic Models

Safety validation depends on a simple but demanding premise: before we deploy a system into the real world, we should test it in a controlled model of the world. That model does not need to capture every microscopic detail of reality. It does need to capture the scenarios that matter for the safety question at hand.

System modeling is therefore an engineering discipline of controlled simplification. A useful model is expressive enough to represent the behaviors, uncertainties, and edge cases the system may encounter, but not so complex that it becomes impossible to build, fit, simulate, or understand.

Models differ in transparency:

- A **white-box model** exposes its internal equations.
- A **black-box model** exposes only inputs and outputs.
- A **gray-box model** exposes some internal structure while hiding other parts.

For validation, transparency matters because it affects how much we can reason about the model itself. But even a transparent model is still an approximation. Offline validation should therefore be paired with runtime monitoring, which catches behavior that falls outside the modeled scenarios.

In this framework, system components are represented as probability distributions:

- $\pi(a \mid o)$: the policy, a distribution over actions given observations.
- $T(s' \mid s, a)$: the transition model, a distribution over next states given current state and action.
- $O(o \mid s)$: the observation or sensor model, a distribution over observations given the true state.

For a discrete random variable, a probability mass function assigns probability to individual outcomes:

$$
\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) = 1, \qquad P(x) \in [0, 1] \;\; \forall x
$$

For a continuous random variable, a probability density function assigns density, not direct probability:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x)\,dx = 1, \qquad p(x) \geq 0 \;\; \forall x
$$

$$
P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx
$$

The most common continuous model class is the Gaussian distribution:

$$
p(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

Gaussian models are widely useful, especially for sensor noise, but they are structurally limited: a single Gaussian is unimodal. No choice of $\mu$ and $\sigma$ can make one Gaussian fit a genuinely bimodal dataset. Parameter tuning cannot fix an insufficient model class.

When a simple distribution cannot represent the data, two common strategies are to combine simple distributions or transform a simple distribution into a more complex one.

A Gaussian mixture model represents a distribution as a weighted sum of Gaussian components:

$$
p_{\text{mix}}(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(x;\, \mu_k,\, \sigma_k^2)
$$

subject to:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k = 1, \qquad \pi_k \geq 0 \;\; \forall k
$$

Another strategy is to start with a simple source distribution and transform it. Let $Z \sim p_Z$ and let $X = f(Z)$. If $f$ is invertible and differentiable, and $g = f^{-1}$ is its inverse, then the density of $X$ is:

$$
\boxed{p_X(x) = p_Z\!\left(g(x)\right) \cdot \left|g'(x)\right|}
$$

In multiple dimensions, the derivative becomes the Jacobian determinant:

$$
p_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) = p_{\mathbf{Z}}\!\left(g(\mathbf{x})\right) \cdot \left|\det J_g(\mathbf{x})\right|
$$

where $J_g(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\partial g}{\partial \mathbf{x}}$ is the Jacobian matrix of the inverse. This is the mathematical foundation behind normalizing flows: learn an invertible transformation from a simple distribution, such as a Gaussian, into a complex target distribution.

Real systems involve multiple variables. A joint distribution describes them together, including their relationships. A conditional distribution describes one variable given another.

For discrete variables:

$$
P(Y = y \mid X = x) = \frac{P(X = x,\, Y = y)}{P(X = x)}
$$

For continuous variables:

$$
p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)} = \frac{p(x, y)}{\int p(x, y')\,dy'}
$$

This formula is exactly what the system model uses. The policy, transition model, and sensor model are all conditional distributions.

For continuous multivariate systems, the multivariate Gaussian is a central model class:

$$
p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2} |\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right)
$$

The mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ gives the center. The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ controls spread and correlation. For a two-dimensional variable:

$$
\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \\ \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22} \end{pmatrix}
$$

where:

$$
\Sigma_{ij} = \text{Cov}(x_i, x_j) = \mathbb{E}[(x_i - \mu_i)(x_j - \mu_j)]
$$

The diagonal entries are variances. The off-diagonal entries are covariances. Positive covariance tilts the Gaussian ellipse toward the $y=x$ direction; negative covariance tilts it the other way.

## Learning and Validating Model Parameters

Once we choose a model class, we still need to fit its parameters. Maximum likelihood estimation gives a general principle: choose the parameters that make the observed data most probable.

$$
\hat{\theta} = \arg\max_{\theta} \; p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)
$$

Here, $\mathcal{D} = \{d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_m\}$ is the observed dataset and $\theta$ is the parameter vector.

Under the i.i.d. assumption, the dataset likelihood factors into a product:

$$
p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) = p(d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_m \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} p(d_i \mid \theta)
$$

Products of many small probabilities are numerically fragile, so MLE is usually written as a log-likelihood objective:

$$
\hat{\theta} = \arg\max_{\theta} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log p(d_i \mid \theta)
$$

<details><summary>Many optimizers minimize rather than maximize, so implementations often minimize the negative log-likelihood:</summary>

```julia
struct MaximumLikelihood
    likelihood    # Function: (x, θ) → probability distribution over y
    optimizer     # Optimization algorithm (e.g., gradient descent)
end

function fit(algorithm::MaximumLikelihood, data)
    # Data is a list of (x, y) pairs
    objective(θ) = -sum(logpdf(algorithm.likelihood(x, θ), y)
                        for (x, y) in data)
    # Minimize negative log-likelihood = maximize log-likelihood
    return optimize(objective, algorithm.optimizer)
end
```

</details>

Ordinary least squares falls out as a special case of MLE. Suppose:

Model: $Y_i | X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(f_\theta(X_i),\, \sigma^2)$

The log-likelihood is:

$$
\hat{\theta} = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^m \log \left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(Y_i - f_\theta(X_i))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\right]
$$

Dropping constants that do not affect the optimizer leaves:

$$
\boxed{\hat{\theta}^{\text{MLE}} = \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^m \left(Y_i - f_\theta(X_i)\right)^2}
$$

This conclusion is significant: squared error is not just a convenient loss. It encodes the assumption that the conditional output noise is Gaussian with constant variance.

Bayesian parameter learning takes a different view: parameters are uncertain quantities, so learning should produce a distribution over parameters. Instead of asking for the single best value of $\theta$, Bayesian learning asks for the posterior distribution:

$$
P(\theta \mid D)
$$

Bayes' rule gives:

$$
P(\theta \mid D) \propto P(D \mid \theta) P(\theta)
$$

The posterior is proportional to the likelihood multiplied by the prior. The difficulty is the missing normalizing denominator:

$$
P(D) = \int P(D \mid \theta) P(\theta) d\theta
$$

For realistic continuous models, this integral is often intractable. Probabilistic programming addresses this problem by letting engineers write down the prior and likelihood directly, then delegating posterior inference to a sampling algorithm.

<details><summary>In Julia, Turing.jl is one such framework:</summary>

```julia
# Conceptual Turing.jl model block
@model function my_model(x_data, y_data)
    # 1. Define Prior
    θ ~ MvNormal(zeros(3), 10.0 * I)  # Weak prior centered at 0 with large variance

    # 2. Define Likelihood
    for i in 1:length(x_data)
        # Assuming y is linear function of x with additive noise
        μ = θ[1] * x_data[i] + θ[2]
        σ = exp(θ[3])
        y_data[i] ~ Normal(μ, σ)      # Condition on observed data
    end
end

# 3. Run Inference
sampler = NUTS()
chain = sample(my_model(xs, ys), sampler, 1000)
```

</details>

Some Bayesian models have analytical solutions because the prior and likelihood form a conjugate pair. A standard example is the Beta-Binomial model. Suppose we want to estimate $\theta$, the probability that two flipped frisbees land facing the same direction. If there are $m$ total flips and $n$ same-direction outcomes, the Binomial likelihood is proportional to:

$$
P(D \mid \theta) \propto \theta^n (1 - \theta)^{m - n}
$$

The conjugate prior for a Bernoulli or Binomial likelihood is the Beta distribution:

$$
\theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta) \implies P(\theta) \propto \theta^{\alpha - 1} (1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1}
$$

Multiplying the prior and likelihood gives:

$$
P(\theta \mid D) \propto \theta^{\alpha + n - 1} (1 - \theta)^{\beta + m - n - 1}
$$

So the posterior is another Beta distribution:

$$
\theta \mid D \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + n, \, \beta + m - n)
$$

Parameter fitting is not the same as model validation. A fitted model must be checked against independent validation data before it can support safety claims. Visual diagnostics are often the first line of defense:

- Overlaying a model PDF on a normalized data histogram.
- Comparing the model CDF with the empirical CDF.
- Using Q-Q plots to compare model quantiles against data quantiles.
- Using calibration plots to check whether predicted probabilities match empirical frequencies.

Q-Q plots are built by choosing probability levels $\alpha \in [0,1]$ and comparing the corresponding model and data quantiles:

$$
x_{\text{model}} = F_{\text{model}}^{-1}(\alpha)
$$

$$
y_{\text{data}} = F_{\text{data}}^{-1}(\alpha)
$$

Numerical metrics help automate and summarize these comparisons. Kullback-Leibler divergence measures how much information is lost when a model distribution $Q$ approximates the data distribution $P$:

$$
D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) = \sum_{x} P(x) \log\left(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right) \quad \text{or} \quad \int p(x) \log\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) dx
$$

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic compares CDFs by measuring their maximum vertical gap:

$$
D_{\text{KS}} = \sup_{x} | F_{\text{data}}(x) - F_{\text{model}}(x) |
$$

A major trap is validating only marginal distributions. A model can match $P(x)$ and $P(y)$ perfectly while failing to capture the joint structure $P(x,y)$. In safety validation, missed correlations can create unrealistic simulations and misleading safety conclusions.

## From Metrics to Specifications

Once the system model is credible, the next input is the property specification: the formal description of what the system is supposed to do.

This distinguishes between two related ideas:

- A **metric** maps behavior to a real number.
- A **specification** maps behavior to a Boolean value.

Because the model is stochastic, repeated simulations produce a distribution over trajectories. Each trajectory can be mapped to a metric value, which means the system induces a distribution over metric values.

The simplest summary is expected value:

$$
\mathbb{E}[M] = \int m \cdot p(m) \, dm
$$

But expected value is often too weak for safety-critical systems. Three different risk distributions can share the same mean while having very different tails.

This motivates **risk metrics**, where higher values indicate worse outcomes.

<details><summary>If "miss distance" is safer when larger, a corresponding risk metric might be "loss of separation":</summary>

```julia
# Standard metric: Miss Distance (higher is better)
miss_distances = [200, 50, 1500, 10]

# Risk metric: Loss of Separation (higher is worse)
initial_separation = 2000
loss_of_separation = initial_separation .- miss_distances
# result: [1800, 1950, 500, 1990]

# Now we can compute VaR and CVaR on `loss_of_separation`
```

</details>

Once metrics are oriented so that higher means worse, we can reason about right-tail risk.

Value at Risk summarizes a risk distribution by its quantile. For a risk metric $R$ and confidence level $\alpha \in [0,1]$:

$$
\text{VaR}_\alpha(R) = \inf \{ r \in \mathbb{R} : P(R \leq r) \geq \alpha \}
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\text{VaR}_\alpha(R) = F_R^{-1}(\alpha)
$$

Conditional Value at Risk averages the tail beyond VaR:

$$
\text{CVaR}_\alpha(R) = \mathbb{E}[R \mid R \geq \text{VaR}_\alpha(R)]
$$

Assuming continuous distributions without point masses at the VaR:

$$
\text{CVaR}_\alpha(R) = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \int_{\text{VaR}_\alpha}^{\infty} r \cdot p_R(r) \, dr
$$

CVaR answers the more safety-relevant question: when we are in the worst tail of outcomes, how severe are those outcomes on average?

A design is Pareto optimal if no other design improves one metric without making another metric worse. The set of all such designs forms the Pareto frontier. To choose a single design from the frontier, engineers often use a composite metric:

$$
M_{\text{composite}}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i M_i(\mathbf{x})
$$

Here, $\mathbf{x}$ is a system design, $M_i(\mathbf{x})$ is the $i$-th metric, and $w_i$ is the importance weight assigned to that metric. Composite metrics are useful, but they introduce two responsibilities. Metrics must be normalized or scaled appropriately, and weights must reflect human values, regulatory tolerance, operational goals, and domain judgment.

Preference elicitation helps determine those weights. Instead of asking a person directly for values, the system presents two options and asks which one the user prefers. Each answer becomes a mathematical constraint on the hidden weights.

Suppose the user compares two candy bags:

$$
A = (a_1, a_2, a_3), \qquad B = (b_1, b_2, b_3)
$$

If the user prefers $A$ over $B$, the model assumes that the weighted utility of $A$ exceeds the weighted utility of $B$:

$$
w_1 a_1 + w_2 a_2 + w_3 a_3 > w_1 b_1 + w_2 b_2 + w_3 b_3
$$

If the weights satisfy:

$$
w_1 + w_2 + w_3 = 1
$$

then only two weights need to be represented explicitly, because:

$$
w_3 = 1 - w_1 - w_2
$$

For example, with:

$$
A = (1, 3, 6), \qquad B = (7, 1, 2)
$$

the preference $A \succ B$ gives:

$$
1 w_1 + 3 w_2 + 6(1 - w_1 - w_2) > 7 w_1 + 1 w_2 + 2(1 - w_1 - w_2)
$$

Simplifying:

$$
-5w_1 - 3w_2 + 6 > 5w_1 - w_2 + 2
$$

$$
4 > 10w_1 + 2w_2
$$

$$
5w_1 + w_2 < 2
$$

This inequality defines a half-space in the feasible weight region. Repeating this process with new pairwise comparisons intersects more half-spaces and shrinks the remaining region of plausible weights. Practical systems often use probabilistic models, often Bayesian ones, where each answer updates a distribution over possible preferences instead of imposing an absolute constraint.

## Logical and Temporal Specifications

Weighted metrics produce real numbers. Logical specifications produce truth values. A logical specification is a formal statement that evaluates to either true or false for a system behavior.

Implication is especially important for system requirements because many requirements are conditional. The statement $P \implies Q$ means "if $P$, then $Q$." It is false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false:

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P \implies Q$ |
| :-: | :-: | :------------: |
|  T  |  T  |       T        |
|  T  |  F  |       F        |
|  F  |  T  |       T        |
|  F  |  F  |       T        |

First-order logic extends propositional logic with variables, predicates, and quantifiers. A variable represents an object in some domain. In a system trajectory, a variable $x$ might represent a state. A predicate is a Boolean-valued function of that variable:

First-order logic is much more expressive than propositional logic, but it still does not naturally represent time ordering. For dynamic systems, sequence matters. That is where temporal logic enters.

Linear Temporal Logic evaluates formulas over a linear sequence of states. The core temporal operators are:

- Always: $\square P$
- Eventually: $\lozenge P$
- Until: $P \mathcal{U} Q$

The formula:

$$
\square P
$$

means that $P$ must hold now and at every future time step. If $P$ fails even once, the formula is false.

The formula:

$$
\lozenge P
$$

means that $P$ must become true at least once now or in the future.

The formula:

$$
P \mathcal{U} Q
$$

means that $Q$ must eventually become true, and $P$ must remain true until that happens.

Signal Temporal Logic extends temporal logic to handle continuous signals and bounded time intervals. A signal is a real-valued function over time. Instead of defining an abstract proposition such as "safe," STL often defines a predicate by comparing a signal to a numerical threshold:

$$
\mu_c(s_t) = s_t \geq c
$$

STL also lets temporal operators apply over explicit time windows:

$$
\square_{[a, b]} P
$$

This means that $P$ must hold throughout the inclusive interval $[a, b]$. What happens before $a$ or after $b$ is irrelevant to this particular formula.

For the pendulum, tipping over means exceeding a 45 degree angle from vertical. The specification is therefore:

$$
\psi = \mathbf{G}_{[0, d]}\, \big(|\theta_t| < \tfrac{\pi}{4}\big)
$$

A trajectory satisfies the specification if:

$$
\tau \models \mathbf{G}_{[0,d]}\,(|\theta_t| < \tfrac{\pi}{4}) \iff \forall\, t \in \{0, 1, \ldots, d\}:\; |\theta_t| < \frac{\pi}{4}
$$

It violates the specification if:

$$
\tau \not\models \psi \iff \exists\, t \in \{0, 1, \ldots, d\}:\; |\theta_t| \geq \frac{\pi}{4}
$$

The core STL grammar is:

$$
\psi ::= \mu \;\mid\; \neg\psi \;\mid\; \psi_1 \wedge \psi_2 \;\mid\; \mathbf{G}_{I}\,\psi \;\mid\; \mathbf{F}_{I}\,\psi \;\mid\; \psi_1\,\mathbf{U}_{I}\,\psi_2
$$

where $\mu$ is an atomic predicate such as $|\theta_t| - \pi/4 \leq 0$.

<details><summary>In Julia, the pendulum specification can be evaluated directly:</summary>

```julia
# STL formula: G[0,d](|θ| < π/4)
function satisfies_spec(τ, threshold=π/4)
    # τ is a trajectory: list of (s, o, a) tuples
    for (s, o, a) in τ
        θ = s[1]  # angle component of state
        if abs(θ) >= threshold
            return false  # violation found
        end
    end
    return true  # all time steps safe
end

function robustness(τ, threshold=π/4)
    # ρ(τ, ψ) = min_t (π/4 - |θ_t|)
    return minimum(threshold - abs(s[1]) for (s, o, a) in τ)
end
```

</details>

## Robustness: From Truth Values to Margins

Signal Temporal Logic is more realistic for physical systems than pure LTL because it can express numerical thresholds and deadlines directly. However, the basic truth value is still binary. A trajectory with vertical separation of 49.9 meters fails the 50-meter requirement just as completely as a trajectory with 0 meters of separation. That brittleness motivates robustness.

Robustness, also called quantitative semantics, assigns a real number to an STL formula evaluated on a trajectory. Instead of returning only true or false, robustness reports how far the trajectory is from the specification boundary.

For a simple threshold predicate:

$$
s \geq c
$$

the robustness is:

$$
\rho(s \geq c) = s - c
$$

If $\rho > 0$, the property is satisfied with positive margin. If $\rho < 0$, the property is violated. The magnitude indicates how comfortably the system satisfies the property or how severely it fails.

For the pendulum:

$$
\rho(\tau, \psi) = \min_{t \in [0,d]} \left(\frac{\pi}{4} - |\theta_t|\right)
$$

Positive robustness means the trajectory satisfies the specification. Negative robustness means it violates the specification. This turns a binary question into a continuous optimization target, which is extremely useful for falsification.

Logical operators become numerical operations on robustness values:

$$
\rho(\neg P) = -\rho(P)
$$

$$
\rho(P \land Q) = \min(\rho(P), \rho(Q))
$$

$$
\rho(P \lor Q) = \max(\rho(P), \rho(Q))
$$

Temporal operators follow the same pattern over time:

$$
\rho(\square P) = \min_t \rho(P_t)
$$

$$
\rho(\lozenge P) = \max_t \rho(P_t)
$$

For an always property, the trajectory's robustness is determined by its worst moment. For an eventually property, robustness is determined by its best moment.

Robustness is useful not only for interpretation, but also for optimization. To find failures, engineers can search for trajectories that minimize robustness. A negative minimum indicates a counterexample: a behavior that violates the specification.

The difficulty is that raw robustness uses `min` and `max`. These functions are not friendly to gradient-based optimization. Smooth robustness replaces sharp `min` and `max` operations with differentiable approximations. A common smooth maximum is:

$$
\text{softmax}(s_1, \dots, s_n; w) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n s_i e^{w s_i}}{\sum_{i=1}^n e^{w s_i}}
$$

The parameter $w$ controls the sharpness. As $w \to \infty$, the largest $s_i$ dominates the exponential weights and the expression approaches the true maximum. As $w \to 0$, all exponentials approach 1, and the expression approaches the arithmetic mean:

$$
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n s_i}{n}
$$

<details><summary>The intuition is visible in code:</summary>

```julia
# The problem with standard max
function raw_robustness(trajectory)
    return maximum(trajectory)
end

# If trajectory = [1, 2, 9, 3], max is 9.
# Changing the 1 to 1.1 does not change the max.
# The gradient through that point is effectively 0.

# Smooth robustness
function smooth_robustness(trajectory, w=10.0)
    numer = sum(s * exp(w * s) for s in trajectory)
    denom = sum(exp(w * s) for s in trajectory)
    return numer / denom
end

# Changing any point slightly can slightly change the output.
# The optimizer receives a more informative gradient.
```

</details>

This is where formal methods connect directly to optimization and machine learning. The specification defines the safety property. Robustness turns the property into a numerical objective. Smooth robustness makes that objective differentiable enough to support gradient-based search for failures or safer trajectories.

## Validation Algorithms

Validation algorithms turn models and specifications into evidence. We organize them into three broad families: failure analysis, formal methods, and complementary methods such as explainability and runtime monitoring.

Falsification asks whether there exists a trajectory that violates the specification:

$$
\tau \not\models \psi
$$

Naive falsification can be implemented by simulation.

<details><summary>Generate rollouts, evaluate the specification, and keep any failure trajectory:</summary>

```julia
# Parameters
N = 30              # number of rollouts
σ = 0.3             # perception noise standard deviation (high initially)
threshold = π/4     # STL specification threshold

# Run rollouts
trajectories = [rollout(system, depth=20) for _ in 1:N]

# Falsification: find any failure
failures = filter(τ -> !satisfies_spec(τ, threshold), trajectories)
println("Falsification succeeded: ", !isempty(failures))
```

</details>

The naive Monte Carlo estimate of failure probability is:

$$
\hat{P}_f^{\text{MC}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}[\tau^{(i)} \not\models \psi]
$$

where $\tau^{(i)} \sim P_S$ is sampled from the system's nominal trajectory distribution.

If the demo produces 6 failures out of 30 rollouts, the estimate is:

$$
\hat{P}_f = \frac{\text{failure trajectories}}{\text{total trajectories}} = \frac{6}{30} \approx 0.2
$$

This works when failures are common. It breaks down when failures are rare. The standard error is:

$$
\text{SE}[\hat{P}_f^{\text{MC}}] = \sqrt{\frac{P_f(1 - P_f)}{N}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{P_f}{N}} \quad \text{(for } P_f \ll 1\text{)}
$$

The relative standard error is:

$$
\frac{\text{SE}[\hat{P}_f^{\text{MC}}]}{P_f} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N \cdot P_f}}
$$

For safety-critical systems with extremely small acceptable failure probabilities, brute-force simulation becomes computationally infeasible. This motivates rare-event methods such as importance sampling and cross-entropy methods.

The same samples can also approximate the failure distribution:

$$
\hat{P}(\tau \mid \tau \not\models \psi) \approx \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{F}} \delta(\tau)
$$

where $\mathcal{F} = \{\tau^{(i)} : \tau^{(i)} \not\models \psi\}$ is the set of failure trajectories.

Formal methods aim to prove properties rather than sample examples. Reachability analysis computes the set of states a system can reach from an initial set. If no reachable state intersects the unsafe region, the system is safe under the model assumptions.

For a simple linear system:

$$
x_{t+1} = x_t + 2, \qquad y_{t+1} = y_t + 1
$$

with initial set $X_0 = [x_{\min}, x_{\max}] \times [y_{\min}, y_{\max}]$, the reachable set at time $t$ is:

$$
\mathcal{R}_t = [x_{\min} + 2t,\; x_{\max} + 2t] \times [y_{\min} + t,\; y_{\max} + t]
$$

For the nonlinear pendulum, exact reachable sets are harder because the dynamics include $\sin(\theta)$. Algorithms compute conservative over-approximations:

$$
\mathcal{R}_{t+1} \supseteq \{f(s, a) : s \in \mathcal{R}_t,\; a \in \mathcal{A}\}
$$

with approximation error:

$$
\mathcal{R}_t^{\text{computed}} = \mathcal{R}_t^{\text{true}} \oplus \mathcal{B}(\epsilon_t)
$$

This conservatism is useful but costly. If the over-approximation stays out of the unsafe set, safety is guaranteed. If it intersects the unsafe set, the algorithm may not know whether the system is genuinely unsafe or the approximation is too loose.

Neural network verification is a related reachability problem. A neural network maps an input set to an output set:

$$
\mathcal{Y} = f_\theta(\mathcal{X}) = f_L \circ f_{L-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_1(\mathcal{X})
$$

where each layer is:

$$
f_l(\mathbf{z}) = \sigma(W_l \mathbf{z} + b_l)
$$

If the output set $\mathcal{Y}$ excludes unsafe outputs for every input in $\mathcal{X}$, the network is verified over that input region.

Explainability asks why a system fails. Runtime monitoring complements offline validation. A system may pass tests and proofs under known assumptions, then encounter an out-of-distribution scenario in deployment. A runtime monitor evaluates behavior while the system operates and can flag uncertainty, trigger fallback logic, or hand control to a human.
