# Beyond Simulation: Falsification and Validation of Complex Autonomous Systems

Modern cyber-physical and intelligent systems—subsurface decision-making AI—are often too complex to validate by inspection alone. Individual components like controllers, perception models, and formal specifications might appear reasonable in isolation, yet the closed-loop system can still fail under specific combinations of initial conditions and random disturbances.

Falsification addresses this problem directly. Instead of attempting the often impossible task of proving that a system is always correct (verification), falsification actively searches for concrete scenarios where the system violates a required safety property. It ensures that rare or catastrophic failures surface before deployment, providing actionable evidence to guide design revisions, policy updates, or risk acceptance.

The practical recipe is consistent across the methods. Define the specification, expose the simulator's randomness as disturbances, generate trajectories, score them with robustness and likelihood, and choose a search method appropriate to the simulator interface. Optimization works when complete trajectories can be proposed and scored. Planning works when step-wise control is available. AST adds adversarial policy learning and likelihood-aware reward design. Sampling methods then move beyond single counterexamples to characterize the distribution of failures itself.

## 1. Falsification Through Optimization: Finding Failures Before Deployment

Modern cyber-physical and intelligent systems are often too complex to validate by inspection alone. A controller, an environment model, a sensor pipeline, and a formal specification may each look reasonable in isolation, yet the closed-loop system can still fail under specific combinations of initial conditions and random disturbances. Falsification addresses this problem directly: instead of trying to prove that a system is always correct, it searches for concrete scenarios where the system violates a required property.

This explains the progression from simple Monte Carlo falsification to disturbance-based modeling, fuzzing, and optimization-based falsification. The central idea is that failures are easier to find when the randomness inside a simulation is made explicit. Once initial states and disturbances become searchable variables, falsification can move from passive sampling to directed optimization.

### What Falsification Means

Falsification is the systematic search for counterexamples: trajectories, inputs, prompts, or scenarios that cause a system to violate a specification. In formal validation, this complements verification. Verification tries to prove that a system satisfies a property; falsification tries to disprove that claim by finding at least one failure.

In a simulation workflow, falsification has a practical safety purpose. Rare or catastrophic failures should surface before deployment, not after the system is operating in the real world. A counterexample can then inform concrete decisions:

| Failure pattern                           | Possible response                    |
| :---------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------- |
| Perception or sensing errors dominate     | Improve sensors or perception models |
| The agent behaves unexpectedly            | Revise the policy or controller      |
| The specification was wrong               | Revise the requirement               |
| A human operator could mitigate the issue | Update training or procedures        |
| The risk is acceptable                    | Document the known limitation        |
| The system is fundamentally inadequate    | Abandon or redesign the project      |

The key point is that a failure trajectory is not just a negative test result. It is evidence that can guide design, policy, requirements, training, or risk acceptance.

### Direct Falsification

The simplest falsification strategy is direct sampling. Run the system many times under the nominal model, monitor each trajectory against a specification, and keep the trajectories that fail.

In notation, direct falsification returns the set

```math
\mathcal{F} = \left\{ \tau_i \;\middle|\; i \in \{1,\ldots,m\},\; \text{failure}(\tau_i, \varphi) = \text{true} \right\}
```

where $m$ is the number of sampled rollouts, $T$ is the trajectory depth, $\tau_i$ is the $i$-th simulated trajectory, and $\varphi$ is the property being checked.

<details><summary>A conceptual implementation looks like this:</summary>

```julia
function falsify(system, spec, depth, m)
  taus = [rollout(system, depth) for _ in 1:m]
  return filter(τ -> failure(τ, spec), taus)
end
```

</details>

This method is attractive because it makes almost no additional assumptions. If failures are common, direct falsification works well. For example, an inverted pendulum with high perception noise may produce many failed rollouts, making it easy to collect counterexamples.

The weakness appears when failures are rare. If the perception noise is reduced toward nominal levels, the same number of rollouts may return no failures at all. Increasing $m$ helps only linearly, so direct sampling quickly becomes impractical when the true failure probability is very small.

### Why Rare Events Break Naive Sampling

Let $P_{\text{fail}}$ be the probability that a single nominal rollout fails. Let $K$ be the index of the first rollout that fails. Under independent rollouts, $K$ follows a geometric distribution.

The expected number of rollouts before the first failure is

$$
\mathbb{E}[K] = \frac{1}{P_{\text{fail}}}
$$

This reciprocal relationship explains the rare-event problem. If $P_{\text{fail}} = 0.2$, a failure appears after about five rollouts on average. If $P_{\text{fail}} = 10^{-9}$, the expected wait is about $10^9$ rollouts.

The probability of seeing no failure in $m$ attempts is $(1 - P_{\text{fail}})^m$. So an empty result from direct falsification is not proof of safety. It may simply mean that the sampling process did not hit a rare failure. This motivates methods that search the simulation space more deliberately.

### Making Randomness Explicit With Disturbances

The next step is to expose every source of randomness in the system as an explicit disturbance. Instead of treating the agent, environment, and sensor as black-box stochastic modules, each module is rewritten as a deterministic function plus a disturbance distribution.

For a sensor model, the general rewrite is

$$
o = O(s, x_o), \quad x_o \sim \mathcal{D}_o(\cdot \mid s)
$$

Here $s$ is the true system state, $o$ is the observation seen by the agent, $x_o$ is a sensor disturbance, $O$ is a deterministic sensor function, and $\mathcal{D}_o$ is the sensor disturbance distribution.

For the inverted pendulum, this can be the additive Gaussian observation model

$$
\tilde{s} = s + x_o, \quad x_o \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)
$$

This is distributionally equivalent to sampling $\tilde{s} \sim \mathcal{N}(s, \Sigma)$, but it names the random noise explicitly.

The same pattern applies to the agent and environment:

$$
a = \pi(o, x_a), \quad x_a \sim \mathcal{D}_a(\cdot \mid o)
$$

$$
s' = f(s, a, x_e), \quad x_e \sim \mathcal{D}_e(\cdot \mid s, a)
$$

The full per-step disturbance is packaged as a single vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_a, x_e, x_o)$. This rewrite does not change the nominal behavior of the simulator. It is bookkeeping. But it is powerful bookkeeping: if the disturbances are recorded, scored, sampled differently, or optimized over, then falsification gains direct control over the randomness that drives failures.

<details><summary>One disturbance-aware simulation step can then be written as:</summary>

```julia
function step(system, s)
    xo = rand(Do(s))                    # sample sensor disturbance
    o  = sensor(system, s, xo)          # o = O(s, xo)

    xa = rand(Da(o))                    # sample agent disturbance
    a  = agent(system, o, xa)           # a = π(o, xa)

    xe = rand(De(s, a))                 # sample environment disturbance
    s′ = environment(system, s, a, xe)  # s′ = f(s, a, xe)

    x = Disturbance(xa, xe, xo)
    return s′, o, a, x
end
```

</details>

For deterministic modules, such as a deterministic pendulum policy or deterministic physics model, the corresponding disturbance distribution can be degenerate or ignored. The uniform interface still matters because it allows the same falsification algorithms to apply across deterministic and stochastic systems.

### Trajectory Distributions

Once disturbances are explicit, a rollout can be described by a trajectory distribution. A trajectory distribution packages three ingredients:

$$
\mathcal{P} = \left( p_S,\; \{\mathcal{D}_t\}_{t=1}^{T},\; T \right)
$$

Here $p_S$ is the initial-state distribution, $\mathcal{D}_t$ is the disturbance distribution at time $t$, and $T$ is the trajectory depth.

Sampling a trajectory follows the generative process

$$
s_0 \sim p_S, \quad \mathbf{x}_t \sim \mathcal{D}_t(\cdot \mid s_{t-1}, o_t, a_t), \quad (s_t, o_t, a_t) = \text{Step}(s_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t)
$$

The important observation is that if $s_0$ and every disturbance $\mathbf{x}_{1:T}$ are fixed, the trajectory is fully determined:

$$
\tau = \text{Rollout}(s_0, \mathbf{x}_{1:T})
$$

This deterministic view is what later enables optimization. There is no remaining sampling once the initial state and disturbance sequence are chosen.

The nominal trajectory distribution represents the real-world deployment model:

$$
p_{\text{nom}} = \mathcal{P}\bigl(p_S^{\text{nom}},\; \mathcal{D}^{\text{nom}},\; T\bigr)
$$

For the inverted pendulum, the nominal perception noise might be

$$
x_o \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \begin{pmatrix} 0.01 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.01 \end{pmatrix}\right) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \sigma_{\theta} = \sigma_{\omega} = 0.1
$$

<details><summary>And a rollout from a trajectory distribution can be expressed as:</summary>

```julia
function rollout(𝒫)
    T = depth(𝒫)
    s = rand(initialstate_distribution(𝒫))
    τ = Trajectory(s)
    for t in 1:T
        D_t = disturbance_distribution(𝒫, t)
        s, o, a, x = step(system, s, D_t)
        push!(τ, (s, o, a, x))
    end
    return τ
end
```

</details>

This formulation separates the nominal model from the search strategy. Direct falsification samples from $p_{\text{nom}}$. More aggressive methods can sample from another distribution while still evaluating plausibility under $p_{\text{nom}}$.

### Fuzzing

Fuzzing improves falsification efficiency by sampling from a biased trajectory distribution. Instead of drawing rollouts from the nominal distribution, it deliberately exaggerates disturbances that are likely to expose failures.

For example, a pendulum fuzzing distribution might keep the same initial-state distribution and depth as the nominal model, but increase the sensor-noise variance:

$$
\mathcal{P}_{\text{fuzz}} = \left( p_S^{\text{nom}},\; \mathcal{D}^{\text{fuzz}},\; T \right), \quad \mathcal{D}^{\text{fuzz}} \neq \mathcal{D}^{\text{nom}}
$$

The fuzzing procedure is then

$$
\tau_i \sim \text{Rollout}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{fuzz}}), \quad i = 1,\ldots,m; \qquad \mathcal{F} = \{\tau_i \mid \text{failure}(\tau_i, \varphi)\}
$$

For the pendulum, the specific noise inflation is

$$
\mathcal{D}_o^{\text{fuzz}}(s) = \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{\text{fuzz}}^2 I), \quad \sigma_{\text{fuzz}} > \sigma_{\text{nom}} = 0.1
$$

As $\sigma_{\text{fuzz}}$ grows, failures become easier to find. But there is a catch: failures found under extreme fuzzing may be very unlikely under the real-world model. They are valid counterexamples to the simulator, but they may be poor evidence of realistic risk.

That is why nominal likelihood matters. A trajectory should be scored by how likely its initial state and disturbances are under the nominal distribution:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{nom}}(\tau) = p_{S}^{\text{nom}}(s_0) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p^{\text{nom}}(\mathbf{x}_t \mid s_{t-1}, o_t, a_t)
$$

The disturbances may have been sampled from $P_{fuzz}$, but the score uses $p_{nom}$. This separates search from evaluation. Fuzzing can bias the search toward failures while nominal likelihood ranks those failures by plausibility.

<details><summary>Code:</summary>

```julia
pfuzz = PendulumFuzzingDistribution(
    system,
    depth = 41,
    perception_std = σ_fuzz   # larger than nominal 0.1
)

taus = [rollout(pfuzz) for _ in 1:m]
failures = filter(τ -> failure(τ, spec), taus)

# Score each failure under NOMINAL likelihood
scores = [nominal_likelihood(τ, pnom) for τ in failures]
```

</details>

Fuzzing is often a strong practical baseline. It can pass from "no failures found" to "many failures found" with a small amount of domain knowledge. Its limitation is that the bias is usually hand-tuned and open-loop. It does not directly optimize for robustness, likelihood, or the boundary between likely success and likely failure.

### Falsification Through Optimization

Optimization-based falsification takes the disturbance formulation one step further. Instead of sampling the initial state and disturbance sequence, it treats them as decision variables:

$$
\text{decision variables:} \quad s_0 \in \mathcal{S}, \quad \mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_T) \in \mathcal{X}^T
$$

Given $s_0$ and $\mathbf{X}$, the rollout is deterministic. The optimizer can therefore search over the full simulation input vector and evaluate each candidate trajectory.

The first-pass optimization objective is based on STL robustness:

$$
\min_{s_0,\, \mathbf{X}} \;\; \rho\bigl(\tau(s_0, \mathbf{X}),\, \varphi\bigr) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \tau(s_0, \mathbf{X}) = \text{Rollout}(s_0, \mathbf{X})
$$

The robustness value $\rho(\tau, \varphi)$ is a signed measure of specification satisfaction. Positive robustness means the trajectory satisfies the property with margin. Negative robustness means the trajectory violates the property. Minimizing robustness therefore pushes the search toward failure.

When a differentiable objective is useful, a smooth robustness approximation can be used.

The deterministic step in optimization mode can be written as

$$
s_t = f_{\text{step}}(s_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t), \quad t = 1,\ldots,T
$$

<details><summary>and implemented without calls to `rand`:</summary>

```julia
function rollout_deterministic(system, s0, X)
  s = s0
  τ = Trajectory(s)
  for (t, x) in enumerate(X)
    o  = sensor(system, s, x.xo)
    a  = agent(system, o, x.xa)
    s  = environment(system, s, a, x.xe)
    push!(τ, (s, o, a, x))
  end
  return τ
end

function falsify_optimize(system, spec, T)
  # Decision: s0 and X[1:T]
  objective(s0, X) = robustness(rollout_deterministic(system, s0, X), spec)
  return minimize(objective, ...)   # CMA-ES, etc.
end
```

</details>

The benefit is clear: optimization replaces blind waiting with directed search. Instead of hoping a rare failure appears in a Monte Carlo sample, the optimizer actively adjusts initial conditions and disturbances to reduce robustness.

The weakness is also clear. Robustness-only optimization can produce failures by choosing extreme disturbances that live far in the tails of the nominal distribution. Such failures are valid, but they may be implausible and therefore less useful for risk assessment.

The natural remedy is a likelihood-aware objective:

$$
\min_{s_0,\, \mathbf{X}} \;\; \rho(\tau, \varphi) + \lambda \cdot \bigl(-\log \mathcal{L}_{\text{nom}}(\tau)\bigr)
$$

This objective balances two goals: make the trajectory fail, and keep the trajectory likely under the nominal model. The robustness term pushes toward violation; the negative log-likelihood term penalizes unrealistic initial states or disturbances.

### The Core Design Tradeoff

The progression from direct falsification to optimization can be summarized as a tradeoff between simplicity, efficiency, and realism.

Direct falsification is simple and faithful to the nominal distribution, but it performs poorly when failures are rare. Fuzzing improves efficiency by biasing the sampling distribution, but it relies on domain knowledge and can overproduce implausible failures. Optimization makes the search systematic by treating randomness as a decision vector, but it must include likelihood or another plausibility mechanism to avoid unrealistic counterexamples.

All three methods depend on the same conceptual foundation:

1. Define a specification $\varphi$.
2. Generate or construct trajectories $\tau$.
3. Evaluate whether `failure(τ, φ)` is true.
4. Prefer failures that are not only valid, but likely under $p_{\text{nom}}$.

The disturbance formulation is the bridge between these methods. It preserves nominal simulation behavior while exposing the variables that falsification needs to manipulate.

## 2. Falsification Through Planning: From Likelihood-Aware Search to Tree-Based Failure Discovery

Optimization-based falsification makes failures easier to find by turning initial states and disturbances into searchable variables. But this all-at-once view can become high-dimensional quickly: a trajectory of depth $T$ with disturbance dimension $d_x$ already contributes $T \cdot d_x$ decision variables, before even counting the initial state. Planning offers another route. Instead of choosing the full disturbance sequence upfront, it grows partial trajectories step by step, reusing previous work and focusing exploration where failure looks reachable.

This explains how likelihood-aware falsification connects to planning. It starts with trajectory likelihood and the practical objective used in optimization, then shows why planning reframes the same problem as incremental tree search. The core algorithms are RRT, falsification-directed RRT, cost-based search, $A^*$ , and Monte Carlo Tree Search.

### Nominal Trajectory Likelihood

The previous optimization view searched over trajectories. To judge whether a discovered failure is useful, we need to ask whether the trajectory is plausible under the nominal model. That score is the nominal trajectory likelihood, written as $p(\tau)$ or $\mathcal{L}_{\text{nom}}(\tau)$.

The key modeling assumption is that a trajectory is generated by sampling an initial state and then sampling one disturbance per time step. Once those random choices are fixed, the dynamics are deterministic. The product form is:

$$
p(\tau) = p_S(s_0) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid s_{t-1}, o_t, a_t)
$$

Here $p_S(s_0)$ is the initial-state density, $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the disturbance at time $t$, and $T$ is the trajectory depth. The disturbance may include agent, environment, and sensor components:

$$
p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \cdot) = p(x_{a,t} \mid o_t)\, p(x_{e,t} \mid s_{t-1}, a_t)\, p(x_{o,t} \mid s_{t-1})
$$

Multiplying many small probabilities is numerically unstable, so implementations normally work in log-space:

$$
\log p(\tau) = \log p_S(s_0) + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid s_{t-1}, o_t, a_t)
$$

<details><summary>A direct implementation accumulates the log-density of the initial state and then the log-density of each disturbance component:</summary>

```julia
function logpdf_trajectory(τ, pnom)
    ll = logpdf(initialstate_distribution(pnom), τ.s0)
    for t in 1:depth(pnom)
        x = τ.disturbances[t]
        ll += logpdf(Da(τ.o[t]), x.xa)
        ll += logpdf(De(τ.s[t], τ.a[t]), x.xe)
        ll += logpdf(Do(τ.s[t]), x.xo)
    end
    return ll   # sometimes exp(ll) returned as pdf
end
```

</details>

The likelihood is always evaluated under the nominal distribution, even if the trajectory was discovered by fuzzing, optimization, or planning. That separation matters: a search method may intentionally bias exploration, while the final score should still measure real-world plausibility.

### The Likelihood-Aware Objective

Robustness-only falsification can find failures that are technically valid but extremely unlikely. For example, an optimizer can push perception noise deep into the tail of the distribution and produce a failure that would almost never happen under deployment conditions. Likelihood-aware falsification corrects this by preferring likely failures over arbitrary failures.

The ideal objective is piecewise:

$$
J(\tau) = \begin{cases} -p(\tau) & \text{if } \tau \notin \psi \quad \text{(failure)} \\ \rho(\tau, \psi) & \text{if } \tau \in \psi \quad \text{(success)} \end{cases}
$$

For failures, minimizing $J$ maximizes likelihood because the objective is $-p(\tau)$. For successes, minimizing $J$ reduces robustness and pushes the trajectory toward the failure boundary. The required ordering is:

$$
J(\tau_{\text{fail}}) < J(\tau_{\text{success}}) \quad \forall \tau_{\text{fail}} \notin \psi,\; \tau_{\text{success}} \in \psi
$$

If this ordering holds globally, then the minimum of the objective is the most likely failure:

$$
\tau^* = \arg\min_{\tau} J(\tau) \;\Rightarrow\; \tau^* \notin \psi \text{ and } p(\tau^*) = \max_{\tau \notin \psi} p(\tau)
$$

This objective is theoretically clean, but it is hard to use directly. Raw likelihoods can be tiny, such as $10^{-100}$, which creates numerical problems for optimizers. Switching to log-likelihood fixes the scale problem, but it can break the sign-based ordering between failures and successes because $-\log p(\tau)$ is often positive.

### The Practical Combined Objective

In practice, falsification often uses a single weighted objective:

$$
J(\tau) = \rho(\tau, \psi) - \lambda \cdot \log p(\tau)
$$

Equivalently, this trades off robustness against negative log-likelihood. The optimization problem is:

$$
\min_{s_0,\, \mathbf{X}} \; J\bigl(\text{Rollout}(s_0, \mathbf{X})\bigr)
$$

Here $s_0$ and the disturbance sequence $\mathbf{X}$ are the decision variables. The rollout is deterministic once those variables are chosen.

The parameter $\lambda$ controls the balance:

- If $\lambda$ is too small, the optimizer may find failures that are unlikely.
- If $\lambda$ is too large, the optimizer may remain close to nominal behavior and fail to discover violations.
- There is no universal value; it must be tuned to the system and specification.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```julia
objective(params) = begin
    s0, X = unpack(params)
    τ = rollout_deterministic(system, s0, X)
    ρ = robustness(τ, spec)           # or smooth_robustness
    ll = logpdf_trajectory(τ, pnom)
  return ρ - λ * ll
end

result = optimize(objective, optimizer)  # Optim.jl, etc.
```

</details>

This formulation is stable and easy to plug into standard optimizers. It does not guarantee the globally most likely failure, but it gives a practical search objective that combines "make the system fail" with "keep the trajectory plausible."

### Choosing an Optimizer

The optimizer operates over:

$$
\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \eta \nabla_\theta J\bigl(\text{Rollout}(\theta_k)\bigr), \quad \theta = (s_0, \mathbf{X})
$$

This local descent update is useful when the simulator, robustness calculation, and log-likelihood are differentiable. First-order methods such as gradient descent or Adam, and second-order methods such as L-BFGS, can then exploit gradient information.

When the simulator is a black box, gradient-based updates are unavailable. Zero-order methods use only objective evaluations:

$$
\theta_{k+1} = \text{Update}\bigl(\theta_k,\; J(\text{Rollout}(\theta_k)),\; \text{neighborhood samples}\bigr)
$$

Examples include Nelder-Mead and Hooke-Jeeves. These methods are slower in high dimensions, but they apply to opaque simulators as long as the simulator can produce a trajectory and the trajectory can be scored.

Population methods sit between local and global search. They maintain many candidate trajectories at once, which can help discover multiple failure modes. In the pendulum example, a local method may find one tip-over direction, while a population method can find both.

The main distinction is practical:

- Use gradients when the full rollout is differentiable and the implementation exposes derivatives.
- Use zero-order methods when the simulator is a black box.
- Use population methods when multimodal search matters and the cost of many evaluations is acceptable.

### Why Planning Enters the Picture

Optimization chooses the full initial state and disturbance sequence at once. The dimension of that search space is:

$$
\dim(\text{search space}) = d_s + T \cdot d_x
$$

For a pendulum with $d_s = 2$, $d_x = 2$, and $T = 21$, that is already 44 dimensions. With $T = 41$, it becomes 84 dimensions. Larger systems can easily reach hundreds or thousands of variables.

Planning changes the structure of the search. Instead of proposing a complete trajectory from scratch, it builds a prefix:

$$
\tau_{1:t} = \text{Rollout}_{1:t}(s_0, \mathbf{x}_{1:t}), \quad t = 1, 2, \ldots, T
$$

Each extension adds one disturbance and one transition. This lets the algorithm reuse partial trajectories, branch from promising states, and avoid throwing away all previous work after an unproductive rollout.

Planning does not remove the difficulty of falsification. It organizes the search so that high-dimensional trajectory construction becomes incremental.

### Tree Search as Select and Extend

The central planning abstraction is a search tree:

- A node is a reachable state $s$.
- An edge is a disturbance-induced transition.
- The root is the initial state.
- The depth of a node is the number of simulation steps in the partial trajectory.

One tree edge corresponds to one disturbance-aware step:

$$
s' = f_{\text{step}}(s, \mathbf{x}), \quad o = O(s, x_o),\; a = \pi(o, x_a)
$$

The generic loop is:

$$
\text{Tree}_{k+1} = \text{Extend}\bigl(\text{Tree}_k,\; \text{Select}(\text{Tree}_k)\bigr)
$$

Different planning algorithms mainly differ in how they implement `select` and `extend!`. RRT, $A^*$ , and MCTS all fit this template, even though they use different priorities and heuristics.

### RRT: Broad Exploration

Rapidly Exploring Random Trees (RRT) grow a tree by repeatedly sampling a goal state, selecting the existing node closest to that goal, and extending from that node.

The Select step is:

$$
s^* = \arg\min_{s \in \text{Tree}} d(s,\; s_{\text{goal}}), \quad s_{\text{goal}} \sim p_{\text{goal}}(s)
$$

The Extend step samples a nominal disturbance and advances the simulator:

```math
s' = f_{\text{step}}(s^*, \mathbf{x}), \quad \mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{D}^{\text{nom}}(\cdot \mid s^*)
```

<details><summary>A vanilla RRT iteration looks like this:</summary>

```julia
function rrt_select_extend!(tree)
    s_goal = rand(state_space)                    # uniform goal
    s_star = argmin(s -> distance(s, s_goal), nodes(tree))
    x = rand(disturbance_distribution_nominal(s_star))
    s_prime = step(system, s_star, x)
    add_child!(tree, s_star, s_prime, x)
end
```

</details>

Vanilla RRT is good at exploration, but it is not automatically good at falsification. If goals are sampled uniformly across the state space and disturbances are sampled from the nominal distribution, the tree may spend most of its effort exploring safe nominal behavior. In the continuum world example, this means the tree tends to move toward the green goal region rather than the red failure region.

### Failure-Directed RRT

RRT becomes much more useful for falsification when its goal sampling and extension are aimed at failures. If the failure region is known, sample the goal from that region:

$$
s_{\text{goal}} \sim \text{Uniform}(\mathcal{S}_{\text{fail}})
$$

This works well for spatial failures, such as entering an obstacle. It is less direct for temporal specifications, where failure may depend on a full trajectory rather than a single state set.

The Extend step can also become goal-directed. Instead of accepting the first sampled disturbance, sample several candidate disturbances and choose the one whose successor state lands closest to the sampled goal:

$$
\mathbf{x}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}_{\text{cand}}} d\bigl(f_{\text{step}}(s^*, \mathbf{x}),\; s_{\text{goal}}\bigr)
$$

<details><summary>The corresponding algorithm is:</summary>

```julia
function extend_toward_goal!(tree, s_star, s_goal; N=100)
    best_x, best_s = nothing, nothing
    best_dist = Inf
    for _ in 1:N
        x = rand(disturbance_distribution_nominal(s_star))
        s_prime = step(system, s_star, x)
        d = distance(s_prime, s_goal)
        if d < best_dist
            best_dist, best_x, best_s = d, x, s_prime
        end
    end
    add_child!(tree, s_star, best_s, best_x)
end
```

</details>

This turns RRT from broad exploration into directed failure search. The tradeoff is computational: more disturbance samples per extension produce better steering, but each extension costs more.

There is also a conceptual limitation. Steering toward the nearest failure region is not the same as finding the most likely failure. A short geometric path to failure may require extreme disturbances, while a more plausible failure may follow nominal behavior for most of the trajectory and deviate only near the end.

### Cost-Based Search and $A^*$

Planning can incorporate objectives directly into the Select step. Instead of choosing the node closest to a random goal, select the node with the lowest estimated total cost:

$$
f(n) = g(n) + h(n, s_{\text{goal}})
$$

$$
n^* = \arg\min_{n \in \text{Tree}} f(n)
$$

Here $g(n)$ is the cost already paid to reach node $n$, and $h(n, s_{\text{goal}})$ is a heuristic estimate of the cost remaining to reach the goal.

For shortest-path search, the costs are:

$$
g(n) = \text{pathlength}(\text{root} \to n), \quad h(n) = \|s_n - s_{\text{goal}}\|_2
$$

For most-likely-failure search, the current cost can use negative log-likelihood of the partial trajectory:

$$
g(n) = -\log p(\tau_{1:t}(n)) + C, \quad h(n) \approx \hat{h}_{\text{nll}}(n, s_{\text{goal}})
$$

The constant $C$ keeps costs positive. This matters because log-likelihood can be positive when densities exceed one, and planning algorithms typically assume positive costs to avoid pathological looping behavior.

When the state and action spaces are discrete and the heuristic is admissible, this becomes $A^*$:

$$
h(n) \leq h^*(n) \quad \forall n
$$

An admissible heuristic never overestimates the true remaining cost. Under the right discrete conditions, A\* can guarantee an optimal shortest path or, with an appropriate likelihood-based cost, an optimal most-likely failure. In continuous RRT-style settings, those guarantees generally do not apply.

### Monte Carlo Tree Search

Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) also fits the Select and Extend framework, but it maintains statistics at each node. Each node has:

- $Q(n)$: a value estimate, where lower is better in this minimization setting.
- $N(n)$: a visit count.

MCTS balances exploration and exploitation. It should deepen branches that look promising, but it should also revisit under-explored branches whose value estimates are uncertain.

In continuous disturbance spaces, a node can have infinitely many possible children. Progressive widening limits how many children are created. If the node already has enough children, selection descends to a child using a lower confidence bound (LCB).

After extension, MCTS initializes the child value and backpropagates the result through the ancestors:

$$
N(n) \leftarrow N(n) + 1
$$

$$
Q(n) \leftarrow \frac{(N(n)-1)\, Q(n) + Q_{\text{new}}}{N(n)}
$$

One way to initialize $Q$ is to run forward rollouts from the new state and average their robustness:

$$
Q_{\text{new}} \approx \frac{1}{M} \sum_{j=1}^{M} \rho\bigl(\text{Rollout}(s_{\text{new}}, \cdot), \psi\bigr)
$$

<details><summary>The core iteration is:</summary>

```julia
function mcts_iteration!(tree, root)
    node = root
    while true
        if num_children(node) <= K * N(node)^α
            break  # extend this node
        end
        node = argmin(c -> LCB(c), children(node))
    end
    # Extend
    x = rand(disturbance_distribution(node))
    child = extend!(tree, node, x)
    Q_new = estimate_Q(child)   # e.g. mean robustness over rollouts
    N(child), Q(child) = 1, Q_new
    # Backpropagate
    while node !== nothing
        N(node) += 1
        Q(node) = moving_average(Q(node), Q_new, N(node))
        node = parent(node)
    end
end
```

</details>

MCTS is useful when falsification needs explicit control over exploration and exploitation. RRT spreads the tree through random targets; MCTS uses statistics gathered during search to decide where more computation should go. Its performance depends heavily on the design of $Q$, the rollout estimator, and hyperparameters such as $K$, $\alpha$, and $C$.

### The Main Design Pattern

The planning view does not replace optimization. It reorganizes the same falsification ingredients:

1. A trajectory is still generated by an initial state and disturbances.
2. Robustness still measures satisfaction or violation of the specification.
3. Nominal likelihood still distinguishes plausible failures from artificial ones.
4. The search method decides how to spend simulation budget.

Optimization spends budget by proposing complete trajectories. Planning spends budget by growing and revisiting partial trajectories. RRT emphasizes coverage, failure-directed RRT adds goal bias, cost-based search incorporates path objectives, $A^*$ provides optimality under discrete admissible conditions, and MCTS balances exploration against exploitation through learned node statistics.

## 3. Adaptive Stress Testing in Practice

AST turns validation into a directed search for plausible failures. Instead of asking whether random simulation happens to reveal a bug, AST asks a sharper question: what is the most likely way this system can fail?

This explains that idea from first principles, connects it to reinforcement learning, tree search, simulator interfaces, objective design, generative modeling, diffusion-based failure sampling, and finally AI's decision problems.

Modern autonomy is attractive because the upside is large. But autonomy also brings brittle machine-learning behavior, distribution shift, and complex interactions that are difficult to validate by inspection.

The practical conclusion is simple: naive validation does not scale to rare catastrophic failures. Falsification methods need to search the simulation space deliberately.

### From Falsification to Reinforcement Learning

Falsification can be cast as a reinforcement learning problem. In ordinary RL, an agent chooses actions to maximize reward. In adversarial falsification, the "agent" is an adversary that chooses disturbances, while the system under test tries to operate normally. The adversary receives higher reward when the system gets closer to violating a specification.

The loop is:

```text
adversary selects disturbance x_t -> system.step() -> reward r_t
```

Mathematically, the adversary can be represented as a policy over disturbances:

$$
r_t = R(s_t, \tau_{1:t}, \psi), \quad \mathbf{x}_t \sim \pi_{\text{adv}}(\cdot \mid s_t)
$$

$$
s_{t+1} = f_{\text{step}}(s_t, \mathbf{x}_t)
$$

Here $\pi_{\text{adv}}$ maps the current state to a disturbance distribution, $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the disturbance at time $t$, $R$ is the adversary reward, and $\psi$ is the safety specification. The important conceptual move is that disturbances become actions.

This gives falsification two advantages from RL. First, RL research focuses heavily on sample efficiency, which matters when each simulation is expensive. Second, a learned adversary policy can generalize across many initial states. Rather than searching from one fixed initial condition, the policy learns which disturbances are dangerous in many states.

Adaptive stress testing adds a likelihood-aware objective. It does not merely seek any failure; it seeks failures that are plausible under the nominal model:

$$
\max_{\pi_{\text{adv}}} \; \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_{\text{adv}}}\bigl[ R_{\text{AST}}(\tau)\bigr], \quad R_{\text{AST}} \propto \mathbb{1}[\tau \notin \psi] \cdot p(\tau)
$$

The indicator $\mathbb{1}[\tau \notin \psi]$ marks specification violation, while $p(\tau)$ scores how likely the trajectory is under the real operating distribution. This separates AST from naive stress testing that simply pushes every disturbance to an extreme.

### The Simulator Determines the Method

The falsification method available in practice depends on the simulator interface. A fully black-box simulator may only accept an initial state and a complete disturbance sequence, then return a trajectory:

$$
\tau = \mathcal{S}(s_0, \mathbf{X}_{1:T})
$$

This interface supports direct sampling, fuzzing, zero-order optimization, and population methods. If gradients are available, first-order and second-order optimization methods become possible.

Tree search and online RL require more access. The simulator must expose a step-wise interface where a method can observe intermediate states and choose the next disturbance:

$$
(s_t, o_t, a_t, \mathbf{x}_t) = \text{step}(s_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t), \quad t = 1,\ldots,T
$$

The distinction matters in real systems. A research simulator may expose clean state transitions, sensor disturbances, and likelihoods. AST often succeeds by adapting to whatever control surface the simulator provides.

### DiFS: Diffusion-Based Failure Sampling

Traditional optimizers and tree search struggle when the disturbance space is very high-dimensional.

Diffusion-based Failure Sampling, or DiFS, addresses this by using conditional generative models to learn where high-risk disturbances live. The method defines a continuous risk metric:

$$
\text{failure} \iff r(\tau) > r_{\text{fail}}
$$

The risk metric may be miss distance, distance to a failure region, ground-contact risk, or another domain-specific proxy. DiFS then trains a conditional diffusion model on disturbance-risk pairs:

```math
\mathcal{D}_k = \bigl\{ (\mathbf{x}_i, r_i) : r_i \text{ from rollout}_i \bigr\}, \quad p_\theta(\mathbf{x} \mid r) \leftarrow \text{train on } \mathcal{D}_k
```

After training on current samples, the model generates new disturbances conditioned on higher risk values. The method raises the target threshold over iterations:

$$
r_{\text{thresh}}^{(k+1)} = Q_{q}\bigl(\{r : (\mathbf{x}, r) \in \mathcal{D}_k\}\bigr)
$$

<details><summary>Conceptually, the algorithm is:</summary>

```julia
D = monte_carlo_disturbance_samples(system)  # compute r for each
r_thresh = initial_threshold(D)

for k in 1:K
    model = train_diffusion(D)                    # learn p(x | r)
    new_x = [sample(model, r ~ Uniform(r_thresh, r_max)) for _ in 1:M]
    new_data = [(x, simulate_risk(system, x)) for x in new_x]
    D = D ∪ new_data
    r_thresh = quantile(D.r, q=0.9)              # raise threshold
end
```

</details>

DiFS bootstraps from ordinary Monte Carlo samples toward increasingly risky regions. In a toy two-dimensional Gaussian setting, it learns to sample the true failure boxes.

The broader point is that generative modeling can become part of the falsification loop. Instead of manually designing every proposal distribution, the system learns how high-risk disturbances are distributed.

### AST as One Part of Safety Engineering

AST is not a complete certification process. It is one tool inside a larger safety lifecycle: requirements, design, simulation, testing, deployment decision, monitoring, and revision. It automates edge-case discovery, but the results still need engineering interpretation.

One practical problem is that validation work becomes stale when the system changes. A small neural-network update or controller change can invalidate previous simulation campaigns:

$$
P_{\text{fail}}(\tau \mid \mathcal{S}_{v2}) \neq P_{\text{fail}}(\tau \mid \mathcal{S}_{v1}) \implies \text{AST results from } v1 \text{ not valid for } v2
$$

The old failures cannot simply be trusted on the new system. But they can warm-start the next validation campaign:

$$
\theta_{\text{search}}^{(v2)} \leftarrow \mathcal{T}\bigl(\theta_{\text{search}}^{(v1)}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{fail}}^{(v1)}\bigr)
$$

Here $\theta_{\text{search}}$ could include optimizer state, MCTS trees, learned adversary policies, or diffusion model weights. The transfer function $\mathcal{T}$ reuses prior validation knowledge without pretending the old result is still a certificate.

This matters because modern ML systems iterate quickly. Validation must become reusable enough to keep up with development.

### AI Decision-Making

The domain changes, but the core structure remains: high-stakes decisions under uncertainty.

This is naturally modeled as a POMDP:

$$
\langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{O}, T, Z, R, \gamma \rangle
$$

$$
b_{t+1}(s') \propto Z(o_{t+1} \mid s', a_t) \int T(s' \mid s, a_t) b_t(s)\, ds
$$

The state $\mathcal{S}$ includes hidden structure, actions $\mathcal{A}$ include injection and measurement, observations $\mathcal{O}$ are sparse, and $b_t$ is the belief over the hidden state.

The computational challenge is severe. AI-style workflows therefore use learned surrogate models:

$$
\hat{T}_\phi(s' \mid s, a) \approx T_{\text{physics}}(s' \mid s, a), \quad \text{speedup} \sim 1000\times
$$

The surrogate is less exact than the physics simulator, but fast enough to support planning and optimization across many scenarios.

The safety risks are concrete. This creates an open validation question: can AST-style disturbance search apply to structure uncertainty? One formulation is:

$$
\mathbf{g}^* = \arg\max_{\mathbf{g} \in \mathcal{G}} \; \mathbb{1}[\eta_{\text{leak}}(\mathbf{g}) > \eta_{\max}] \cdot p(\mathbf{g})
$$

Here $\mathbf{g}$ is a scenario, $\mathcal{G}$ is the uncertainty ensemble, and $p(\mathbf{g})$ is the prior probability. This is the same most-likely-failure idea, but the disturbance is no longer sensor noise. It is the hidden structure of the scenario.

## 4. Sampling Failure Distributions: From Direct Sampling to Smoothed MCMC

Falsification usually starts with a simple objective: find at least one trajectory that violates a specification. That is already useful, but it is not the whole story. Once failures exist, we may want to understand the distribution of failures: which failures are common, which are rare, and how different failure modes compare under the nominal trajectory model.

The failure distribution answers that question. It takes the nominal trajectory distribution and conditions it on specification violation. The result is a probability distribution over only the failing trajectories. This explains how that distribution is defined, why its normalizing constant is hard to compute, and how rejection sampling, Markov Chain Monte Carlo, burn-in, thinning, and robustness-based smoothing can be used to sample from it.

### The Failure Distribution

Let $p(\tau)$ be the nominal trajectory density and let $\tau \notin \psi$ mean that trajectory $\tau$ violates the specification $\psi$. The failure distribution is the conditional distribution of trajectories given failure:

$$
p(\tau \mid \tau \notin \psi) = \frac{p(\tau)\, \mathbb{1}[\tau \notin \psi]}{\displaystyle\int p(\tau')\, \mathbb{1}[\tau' \notin \psi]\, d\tau'}
$$

The numerator keeps the nominal density on failures and assigns zero density to successes:

$$
\text{Numerator}(\tau) = p(\tau)\, \mathbb{1}[\tau \notin \psi] = \begin{cases} p(\tau) & \tau \notin \psi \\ 0 & \tau \in \psi \end{cases}
$$

The denominator is the total probability mass of the failure set:

$$
P(\tau \notin \psi) = \int p(\tau')\, \mathbb{1}[\tau' \notin \psi]\, d\tau'
$$

Conceptually, this is just conditional probability. Practically, it is hard because the denominator requires integrating over every possible trajectory. In high-dimensional systems, that integral is usually intractable.

A one-dimensional Gaussian example gives the right intuition. Suppose a trajectory is just a scalar state:

$$
\tau \notin \psi \iff s < -1, \quad \tau = s \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)
$$

The numerator is the left tail of the standard normal distribution and zero everywhere else. It already has the right shape for the failure distribution. The denominator only rescales that left-tail area so the density integrates to one.

### Unnormalized Failure Density

Because the denominator is usually unavailable, it is useful to separate the shape of the failure distribution from its scale. Define the unnormalized failure density as:

$$
\bar{p}(\tau) = p(\tau)\, \mathbb{1}[\tau \notin \psi]
$$

Then the true failure distribution is:

$$
p(\tau \mid \tau \notin \psi) = \frac{\bar{p}(\tau)}{\int \bar{p}(\tau')\, d\tau'} = \frac{\bar{p}(\tau)}{P(\tau \notin \psi)}
$$

The important point is that $\bar{p}(\tau)$ is often easy to evaluate at a proposed trajectory. We can check whether $\tau$ fails the specification, and we can evaluate the nominal trajectory likelihood $p(\tau)$ using the trajectory model from earlier falsification.

<details><summary>A direct implementation is:</summary>

```julia
function pbar_failure(τ, spec, pnom)
    if !failure(τ, spec)          # τ ∈ ψ (success)
        return 0.0
    end
    return pdf_trajectory(τ, pnom)  # or exp(logpdf_trajectory(...))
end
```

</details>

This function does not know the normalizing constant. It does not need to. Many sampling algorithms only require a density up to proportionality. The rest explains two major families: rejection sampling and MCMC.

### Rejection Sampling by Geometry

Rejection sampling can be understood as a dartboard construction. Draw the curve $\bar{p}(\tau)$ on a board. Throw darts uniformly over a region that sits above the curve. Keep the darts that land below $\bar{p}(\tau)$ and reject the rest. If we drop the accepted darts down to the $\tau$-axis, their horizontal positions follow the normalized target density.

The geometric acceptance condition accepts darts that land under the curve $h \leq \bar{p}(\tau)$. With enough darts, a histogram of the accepted samples converges to the target shape:

$$
\hat{p}_N(\tau) = \frac{1}{N_{\text{accept}}} \sum_{i: \text{accepted}} \mathbb{1}[\tau \in \text{bin}] \xrightarrow{N \to \infty} p(\tau \mid \text{target})
$$

This picture works for Gaussian, bimodal, and irregular densities. Its weakness is waste. Darts above the curve are discarded, and if the enclosing region is much larger than the target density, most proposals are lost.

### Formal Rejection Sampling

The formal version replaces the rectangular dartboard with a proposal distribution $q(\tau)$ and an envelope constant $c$. The proposal must be easy to sample from, and the scaled proposal must dominate the unnormalized target everywhere:

$$
\bar{p}(\tau) \leq c \cdot q(\tau) \quad \forall \tau
$$

Sampling then proceeds in three steps:

1. Draw $\tau \sim q(\tau)$.
2. Draw $r \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$.
3. Accept $\tau$ with probability $\bar{p}(\tau)/(c \cdot q(\tau))$.

The acceptance criterion ensures that we accept $\tau$ with probability proportional to the ratio between the unnormalized target and the envelope. The closer the envelope $c \cdot q(\tau)$ is to $\bar{p}(\tau)$, the fewer samples are rejected. But $c$ must be large enough to cover the target everywhere. If the envelope falls below the target in any region, samples from that region will be underrepresented and the method becomes invalid.

This algorithm highlights the two design problems in rejection sampling: choosing $q$ and choosing $c$. A poor proposal wastes samples. A poor envelope constant either rejects almost everything or breaks correctness.

### Failure Sampling as Rejection Sampling

For the failure distribution, the target is:

$$
\bar{p}(\tau) = p(\tau)\, \mathbb{1}[\tau \notin \psi]
$$

The simplest proposal is the nominal trajectory distribution itself:

$$
q(\tau) = p(\tau), \quad c = 1 \implies P(\text{accept} \mid \tau) = \mathbb{1}[\tau \notin \psi]
$$

This reduces rejection sampling to direct sampling: roll out nominal trajectories and keep only the failures.

This is correct, but it is inefficient when failures are rare. If only one in a million nominal trajectories fails, then almost every proposal is rejected.

A better proposal can shift probability mass toward the failure region. In the one-dimensional Gaussian toy problem with failure threshold near $-1$, a shifted Gaussian proposal can be used:

$$
q(\tau) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_q, \sigma_q^2), \quad \mu_q \approx \tau_{\text{fail threshold}}
$$

The envelope condition still has to hold:

$$
\bar{p}(\tau) \leq c \cdot q(\tau) \quad \forall \tau, \quad c \text{ minimal}
$$

Efficiency can be summarized by the accepted fraction:

$$
\eta = \frac{N_{\text{accepted}}}{N_{\text{proposed}}} \approx \frac{P_{q}(\tau \notin \psi)}{c}
$$

This improves over nominal sampling when $q$ concentrates on likely failures and $c$ is tight. However, designing such a proposal is not automatic. In high-dimensional systems such as pendulum rollouts over long horizons, finding a globally valid $c$ can become impractical.

### Markov Chain Monte Carlo

Markov Chain Monte Carlo takes a different approach. Instead of drawing independent proposals from a global envelope, MCMC builds a chain of correlated samples. Each new state depends on the current state through a proposal kernel $g(\tau' \mid \tau)$.

For a symmetric kernel, such as a Gaussian centered at the current trajectory, the Metropolis acceptance probability is:

$$
\alpha(\tau, \tau') = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\bar{p}(\tau')}{\bar{p}(\tau)}\right)
$$

This rule has a simple intuition:

- If the proposal has higher unnormalized density than the current state, accept it.
- If the proposal has lower density, accept it with probability proportional to the density ratio.

For non-symmetric proposal kernels, the full Metropolis-Hastings correction is:

$$
\alpha(\tau, \tau') = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\bar{p}(\tau')\, g(\tau \mid \tau')}{\bar{p}(\tau)\, g(\tau' \mid \tau)}\right)
$$

A common symmetric kernel is:

$$
g(\tau' \mid \tau) = \mathcal{N}(\tau' \mid \tau, \Sigma_{\text{kernel}})
$$

The kernel covariance $\Sigma_{\text{kernel}}$ controls how far the chain moves at each step. If it is too small, the chain moves slowly and may get trapped in one mode. If it is too large, most proposals may land in low-density or zero-density regions and get rejected.

<details><summary>A basic MCMC failure sampler is:</summary>

```julia
function mcmc_failure_sample(τ_init, pbar, g; n_steps=10000)
    τ = τ_init
    chain = [τ]
    for _ in 1:n_steps
        τ_prime = sample(g, τ)              # propose from kernel
        ratio = pbar(τ_prime) / pbar(τ)     # symmetric g: cancel g terms
        if rand() < min(1.0, ratio)
            τ = τ_prime
        end
        push!(chain, τ)
    end
    return chain
end
```

</details>

MCMC avoids the need to choose an envelope constant $c$. It still only needs the unnormalized density $\bar{p}$. The tradeoff is that samples are correlated, and finite chains may not represent the target distribution well unless the chain has mixed.

### Burn-In and Thinning

The theoretical guarantee for MCMC is asymptotic: under suitable conditions, the chain converges to the target distribution as the number of samples goes to infinity. In practice, chains are finite, and two standard corrections are burn-in and thinning.

Burn-in discards early samples that are strongly influenced by the initial state. This matters especially when the chain starts outside the failure region. Because $\bar{p}(\tau)=0$ for successes, an MCMC chain can spend time wandering or getting stuck before it reaches useful failure support.

Thinning keeps every $m$-th sample to reduce serial correlation.

Burn-in length and thinning interval are heuristics. They do not guarantee that every failure mode has been explored. They only address two finite-sample problems: initialization bias and adjacent-sample correlation.

### Multi-Modal Failures and Robustness Smoothing

Hard failure indicators can make MCMC difficult. Consider a system with two separated failure modes: one at very low state values and another at very high state values. Between them is a success region where $\bar{p}(\tau)=0$. A local Gaussian MCMC kernel may explore one failure mode but fail to cross the zero-density gap to the other mode within any practical number of samples.

This introduces smoothing as a workaround. Define a distance-to-failure function:

$$
\delta(\tau) = \begin{cases} 0 & \tau \notin \psi \text{ (failure)} \\ > 0 & \tau \in \psi \text{ (success)} \end{cases}
$$

A convenient choice uses STL robustness:

$$
\delta(\tau) = \max\bigl(0,\; \rho(\tau, \psi)\bigr)
$$

Here $\rho(\tau,\psi)$ is negative for failures and positive for successes. Taking the maximum with zero gives a distance proxy that is zero exactly on failures and positive for non-failures.

Now replace the hard indicator with a Gaussian density over this distance:

$$
\bar{p}_{\text{smooth}}(\tau) = p(\tau) \cdot \mathcal{N}\bigl(\delta(\tau) \mid 0, \epsilon^2\bigr)
$$

This assigns nonzero weight to near-failures. Those near-failure states form bridges through success regions, allowing the MCMC chain to move between separated failure modes.

The smoothing parameter $\epsilon$ controls the tradeoff:

$$
\epsilon \to 0 \implies \bar{p}_{\text{smooth}} \to p(\tau)\mathbb{1}[\tau \notin \psi], \quad \epsilon \to \infty \implies \bar{p}_{\text{smooth}} \to p(\tau)
$$

Small $\epsilon$ behaves like the hard failure indicator and may still trap the chain. Large $\epsilon$ approaches the nominal distribution and may produce too many non-failure samples. The useful range is empirical: large enough to connect modes, but not so large that the chain ignores failure structure.

After running MCMC on the smoothed density, recover true failure samples by filtering:

$$
\mathcal{S}_{\text{failure}} = \{\tau \in \text{MCMC chain} : \tau \notin \psi\}
$$

This is effectively a two-stage method:

1. Use smoothed MCMC to explore the broader near-failure landscape.
2. Reject non-failures to return to the true failure support.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```julia
function pbar_smooth(τ, spec, pnom, ε)
    δ = max(0.0, robustness(τ, spec))
    return pdf_trajectory(τ, pnom) * pdf(Normal(0, ε), δ)
end

chain = mcmc_failure_sample(τ_init, τ -> pbar_smooth(τ, spec, pnom, ε), g)
failure_samples = filter(τ -> failure(τ, spec), chain)
```

</details>

Smoothing deliberately introduces non-failure samples into the chain. That is not a bug. Those samples are the bridge that lets the chain move between disconnected failure modes. The final rejection step restores the hard failure condition.

### Choosing Between the Methods

The methods form a practical hierarchy.

Direct sampling is the simplest case of rejection sampling with $q=p$ and $c=1$. It is correct and easy to implement, but it fails when failures are rare.

General rejection sampling can be much more efficient if a good proposal distribution and valid envelope constant are available. Its weakness is proposal design. In high-dimensional trajectory spaces, verifying that $\bar{p}(\tau) \leq c \cdot q(\tau)$ everywhere is often the hard part.

MCMC avoids the global envelope problem and only needs density ratios. It is useful when local moves can explore the failure distribution. But it produces correlated samples, needs burn-in and tuning, and may miss modes when failure regions are separated.

Robustness smoothing extends MCMC by softening the hard failure boundary. It lets the chain move through near-failure regions, then filters the result back to true failures. This connects failure distribution sampling to robustness-based falsification: robustness is not only a search objective, but also a way to build usable sampling geometry.
