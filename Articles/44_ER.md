# Beyond Black-Box Models: Explainability, Stress Testing and Runtime Assurance for Safety-Critical AI

Safety-critical AI cannot be justified by a passing test, a strong benchmark score, or a low estimated failure rate alone. Engineers also need to understand what the system has learned, identify the conditions that turn model errors into physical hazards, and detect when the evidence collected offline no longer applies during operation.

The central principle is simple: safety emerges from layers of complementary evidence. Explanation supports diagnosis, reachability connects model behavior to physical consequences, and runtime assurance manages the uncertainty that remains after deployment. This is an end-to-end assurance workflow. It begins with explainability methods that reveal policy behavior and feature influence, moves from attribution toward causal analysis of internal mechanisms, connects perception errors to closed-loop failures through system-level stress testing, and runtime monitors that combine operational-domain checks, uncertainty estimates, known-failure maps, and validated fallback behavior.

An effective safety case for AI must connect four questions: what the model attends to, how its internal or external mechanisms produce a decision, when that decision creates a system-level hazard, and whether the deployed system can recognize when its prior evidence is no longer trustworthy. Explainability, causal intervention, reachability analysis, and runtime monitoring each answer a different part of that chain.

The practical implication is to build assurance as a loop rather than a gate. Engineers should use explanations to form failure hypotheses, test those hypotheses through controlled interventions and closed-loop analysis, convert discovered hazards into monitors and retraining data, and then re-evaluate the complete system for regressions. No layer eliminates residual risk, but together they turn opaque model behavior into evidence that can be inspected, challenged, and improved throughout the system's life cycle.

## 1. Explainability Beyond Pass-or-Fail Validation for Safety-Critical Systems

Validation can tell us that an AI system passed a test, satisfied a formal property, or produced an acceptably low estimated failure probability. It cannot, by itself, tell us why the system behaved correctly. That distinction matters: a model may succeed on every measured criterion while relying on brittle correlations, irrelevant inputs, or decision logic that fails outside the tested conditions.

Explainability closes this gap by turning a binary question—“Does it work?”—into a diagnostic one: “Why does it work, and what causes it to fail?” For engineers working with safety-critical systems, the answer provides more than intellectual reassurance. It helps verify that a policy attends to meaningful features, reveals behavior that aggregate metrics hide, supports debugging, and strengthens the case that the system will generalize beyond its validation set.

No single technique provides a complete explanation. A useful explainability workflow moves through several levels: visualizing the policy, measuring feature importance, capturing feature interactions, approximating opaque logic with interpretable models, generating counterfactuals, and organizing failures into distinct modes. Together, these methods turn a black-box result into evidence that engineers can inspect and act on.

### Start by Looking at the Policy

Policy visualization is often the cheapest and most revealing first step. A deterministic policy maps a state $s$ to an action $a$.

For an inverted pendulum, the state might contain angle $\theta$ and angular velocity $\omega$, while the action is the applied torque. For a collision-avoidance system, the state can include relative altitude, time to collision, and other encounter variables, while actions may include climb, descend, or clear of conflict.

There are three practical levels of visualization.

#### Inspect simulated rollouts

The simplest approach is to simulate the policy and plot its trajectories. A collision-avoidance rollout may reveal two broad strategies: climb above the intruder or descend below it. An inverted-pendulum rollout shows whether the controller holds the pendulum near the upright position and under what circumstances it falls.

Rollouts do not expose the entire policy, but they provide an immediate behavioral sanity check. They also show temporal structure that a static state-action diagram cannot capture.

#### Plot the state-to-action map

When the state space has two or three dimensions, engineers can plot the policy directly. An inverted-pendulum heat map over $(\theta, \omega)$ should show zero torque near $(0,0)$ and restoring torque when the pendulum leans away from vertical. Unexpected discontinuities or asymmetric regions become visually obvious.

Higher-dimensional policies require two-dimensional slices. In ACAS X, fixing some state variables and plotting relative altitude against time to collision can expose meaningful decision boundaries. One such slice revealed an unintuitive “notch”: a no-advisory region close to the intruder. That kind of feature can disappear inside an aggregate safety score but becomes impossible to ignore on a policy plot.

Slices must be interpreted carefully. Their content depends on which variables are held fixed and at what values. A poorly selected slice may miss the policy’s most important structure.

#### Summarize complex policies with state-space partitions

A direct plot is not possible when a policy depends on a high-dimensional state or on history. In that case, the analyst can partition a lower-dimensional projection of the state space into regions, run many trajectories, and record the action taken whenever a trajectory visits each region. The displayed action for each region is simply the one observed most often.

This method works for non-Markovian policies because the policy’s hidden history is already reflected in the rollouts. However, the plot is a summary rather than the policy itself. A cell displays only its most frequent action; it can conceal substantial variation within the region. Cell size, projection, and visitation frequency all affect the result.

Visualization is therefore best treated as an exploratory tool. It tells engineers where to look next, not necessarily why a feature exists.

### Measure Which Features Drive the Output

Feature-importance methods move from behavior to attribution. They ask which pixels, state variables, or disturbances most influence a chosen output. That output might be a steering command, a probability of failure, or a trajectory robustness score $\rho$ measuring how far the system is from violating a specification.

Sensitivity analysis offers three increasingly sophisticated answers: direct perturbation, local gradients, and integrated gradients.

#### Monte Carlo perturbation

The most direct method changes one feature at a time. For feature $i$, hold every other feature at its observed value, resample $x_i$ from its nominal distribution $p_i$, reevaluate the system, and measure the variance of the output:

$$
S_i = \text{Var}_{x_i \sim p_i}\left[ f(x_1, \ldots, x_i, \ldots, x_n) \right]
$$

A large $S_i$ means changes to feature $i$ produce a wide spread of outputs. This definition is intuitive and can be applied to a neural network or a full simulator. For a pendulum, it can identify the disturbances whose variation most changes trajectory robustness.

The cost is substantial. A 64×64 image contains 4,096 pixel features. Repeating a simulation many times for every pixel requires thousands of batches of evaluations. Direct perturbation is useful as a reference method, but it scales poorly.

#### Gradient saliency

When $f$ is differentiable, a saliency map replaces repeated resampling with the local gradient:

$$
\text{Saliency}_i = \left| \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_i} \right|
$$

The gradient measures how quickly the output changes when feature $i$ is nudged at the current input. A single backward pass can compute saliency for every feature, making it dramatically cheaper than one-feature-at-a-time simulation.

A first-order Taylor approximation shows why this is related to perturbation: the output variance induced by a feature is approximately proportional to that feature's nominal variance and to the square of its local gradient. This also exposes the method’s limitation: saliency is local. If the model is saturated at $x$, the gradient may be near zero even when the feature was crucial in moving the model to that region.

SmoothGrad tries to reduce noisy local gradients by averaging saliency over perturbed copies of the input. Its basic attribution is $SG_i = E_{\epsilon \sim N(0,\sigma^2)}[|\partial f(x+\epsilon)/\partial x_i|]$.

#### Integrated gradients

Integrated gradients address saturation by accumulating gradients along a path from a baseline input $x'$ to the actual input $x$:

$$
\text{IG}_i(x) = (x_i - x'_i) \int_{\alpha=0}^{1} \frac{\partial f\bigl(x' + \alpha(x - x')\bigr)}{\partial x_i} \, d\alpha
$$

In practice, the integral is approximated with a finite sequence of interpolated inputs. For an image, $x'$ might be an all-black baseline. Evaluating gradients from $x'$ to $x$ lets a feature receive a large attribution even if the gradient at the final input is flat. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives the method its completeness property: the individual attributions sum to $f(x)-f(x')$.

Integrated gradients do not eliminate judgment. The baseline defines what “absence” or “neutrality” means, and different baselines can produce different explanations. That choice must make sense in the application domain.

#### Sanity-check the explainer

An explanation method can produce a plausible image without explaining the model. Research on sanity checks for saliency maps found that some methods yielded visually similar results even after model weights were randomized. In those cases, the apparent explanation behaved more like an edge detector than a faithful account of learned decision logic.

The practical lesson is simple: explanation tools also require validation. Randomize model parameters, compare different trained models, perturb supposedly important features, and check whether the attribution changes as it should. A compelling heat map is evidence only when it demonstrably depends on the model and output being explained.

### Capture Interactions

Sensitivity methods can miss features whose importance depends on other features. Consider a wildfire model in which either of two neighboring burning cells is sufficient to keep the predicted spread probability at 0.3. Changing either cell alone has no effect because the other burning neighbor remains. A one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis therefore assigns both cells little or no importance, even though the pair clearly drives the result.

Shapley values address this problem by borrowing a credit-allocation principle from cooperative game theory. Features are players, subsets of features are coalitions, and the model output is the coalition’s value. The Shapley value for feature $i$ averages its marginal contribution across every possible coalition of the other features:

$$
\phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(n-|S|-1)!}{n!} \left[ v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S) \right]
$$

Here, $N$ is the set of all $n$ features and $S$ is any subset that excludes $i$. The term $v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)$ measures how much the expected output changes when feature $i$ is added to coalition $S$. The factorial weight is the fraction of feature orderings in which exactly the features in $S$ precede $i$.

The coalition value is obtained by fixing features in $S$ to their observed values and averaging over the remaining features. This construction captures pairwise and higher-order interactions because the contribution of $i$ is evaluated in many different feature contexts. It also gives Shapley values their fairness properties: efficiency, symmetry, the dummy property, and additivity.

#### Approximate an exponential computation

Exact Shapley values require evaluating $2^{n-1}$ coalitions for each feature. A 25-cell wildfire grid already has more than 16 million possible coalitions excluding one target feature, and every coalition value may itself require Monte Carlo sampling. Practical implementations therefore sample subsets. One common approach samples a random ordering of all features and uses those that appear before feature $i$, reproducing the coalition-size weighting in the exact definition.

Increasing $M$ improves the estimate but raises the computational cost. The result is an approximation with sampling error, not an exact attribution.

Feature dependence introduces another challenge. If non-coalition features are sampled independently of the fixed coalition, the resulting combinations may be unrealistic—for example, image pixels that violate the structure of natural images. Conditional Shapley values replace the marginal distribution with $p(x_{\bar{S}} \mid x_S)$, preserving dependencies at the cost of a harder estimation problem.

Shapley values can be especially useful for failure analysis. In the pendulum example, they rank the observation disturbances that contributed most to a failed trajectory. Removing the four highest-ranked disturbances eliminates that failure, connecting attribution to a concrete behavioral check.

### Use Surrogate Models to Expose Decision Logic

Feature importance identifies what matters; it does not necessarily show how a policy uses those features. A surrogate model takes the next step by learning a simpler approximation of the opaque policy.

The basic linear surrogate, including an intercept, is:

$$
\hat{f}(x) = w_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{d} w_j x_j = w_0 + w^\top x
$$

The sign and magnitude of $w_j$ provide a direct account of how feature $j$ affects the surrogate output. A global linear model is easy to understand, but a nonlinear policy may not be well approximated by one set of weights over its entire state space.

#### Explain one decision with LIME

Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations, or LIME, fits a simple model near one point of interest. The procedure samples neighboring states $x'$, labels them with the original policy $\pi(x')$, gives closer samples more weight, and learns a sparse linear approximation:

$$
\hat{w} = \arg\min_{w} \sum_{x' \in \mathcal{N}(x)} \kappa(x, x') \left[ \pi(x') - w^\top x' \right]^2 + \lambda \|w\|_1
$$

The neighborhood $\mathcal{N}(x)$ and kernel $\kappa(x,x')$ define what “local” means. The $L_1$ penalty $\lambda\|w\|_1$ encourages a small number of nonzero weights, making the explanation easier to read.

For an ACAS X policy slice, a local model near the center might show that relative altitude dominates time to collision. A model near the no-advisory boundary may assign substantial weight to both. Neither local explanation claims to describe the entire policy.

The locality kernel is itself an important design choice. A narrow neighborhood improves local specificity but can make the model unstable; a broad neighborhood provides more samples but may average over different decision regimes.

#### Translate the policy into a decision tree

A decision-tree surrogate expresses policy logic as feature thresholds. At every internal node, it selects feature $x_j$ and threshold $\theta$, then follows the left or right subtree according to whether the feature is below or above that threshold.

Each root-to-leaf path becomes a human-readable rule. Tree depth controls a fundamental trade-off: a shallow tree is clear but may miss important boundaries, while a deep tree may closely reproduce the original policy but become too complex to interpret. Polynomial terms create the same tension in linear surrogates: interactions such as altitude multiplied by time to collision can improve fidelity while making individual weights harder to explain.

This limitation must remain explicit. A surrogate explains its approximation of the policy, not the original policy directly. If the surrogate is inaccurate in the region under discussion, its transparent logic can still be a misleading explanation.

### Explain Outcomes with Counterfactuals

Counterfactual explanations ask a question that people naturally use when reasoning about causes: what is the smallest change that would have produced a different result?

For an original input $x$, the counterfactual can be formulated as a constrained optimization problem:

$$
\delta^* = \arg\min_{\delta} \|\delta\| \quad \text{subject to} \quad f(x + \delta) \neq f(x)
$$

In a softer version, the objective balances the size of the change against a loss that encourages the desired output $y*$. The optimal perturbation $\delta*$ should be small while moving the output toward that value. In a collision-avoidance setting, the explanation might say that an intruder 10 feet lower would have changed a climb advisory to descend. For a pendulum failure, it might identify the reduction in an early disturbance needed for recovery.

This form of explanation is contrastive and potentially actionable. Sensitivity says which features can affect an outcome; a counterfactual says what would have needed to change for this outcome to be different.

Minimality alone is not sufficient. There may be several equally small counterfactuals, some physically impossible or operationally meaningless. Useful counterfactuals must respect feasible feature ranges, dependencies, immutable variables, and system dynamics. The selected norm also changes the answer: an $\ell_1$ objective favors sparse changes, while an $\ell_2$ objective may distribute a smaller adjustment across many features.

### Turn Individual Failures into Failure Modes

After falsification or rare-event sampling produces a collection of failed trajectories, engineers still face a practical question: how many distinct kinds of failure are present? Reviewing cases one by one does not reveal whether hundreds of failures are variations of two mechanisms or evidence of dozens.

Failure-mode characterization treats this as a clustering problem. Each failed trajectory becomes a feature vector, and similar trajectories are grouped together. The standard k-means objective is:

$$
\min_{\{C_1,\ldots,C_k\}} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{x \in C_j} \|x - \mu_j\|^2
$$

where $\mu_j = \frac{1}{|C_j|} \sum_{x \in C_j} x$ is the centroid of cluster $j$.

The objective is simple; choosing the representation $x$ is not. For inverted-pendulum failures, clustering on average angle and angular velocity $(\bar{\theta},\bar{\omega})$ can separate falls to the left from falls to the right. Clustering on the full state trajectory may preserve additional temporal information. Action sequences can reveal different controller responses, while disturbance trajectories may or may not produce interpretable groups.

That last case is important: clustering always returns clusters, but the result is not automatically meaningful. Feature selection can matter more than the clustering algorithm. Principal component analysis can reduce a high-dimensional disturbance or state space before clustering, but the resulting axes still require interpretation. The analyst must also choose $k$; too few clusters merge distinct mechanisms, while too many fragment one mechanism into artificial subgroups.

#### Describe failures with temporal logic

Parametric Signal Temporal Logic, or pSTL, offers a more semantic representation. Instead of clustering raw vectors, it describes each trajectory through parameters of a temporal-logic formula. The analyst finds the parameters that best explain each failure trajectory by minimizing its violation of the parameterized specification.

The parameter vector $\pi$ might contain a failure time and a signal threshold. Clustering in this parameter space can separate, for example, failures in which the pendulum falls before a time boundary from those in which it falls afterward. Because each cluster corresponds to a temporal-logic description with concrete parameters, the output can be easier to communicate than a centroid in a 100-dimensional trajectory space.

This semantic gain comes with a dependency on domain knowledge. The analyst must choose the structure of $\varphi(\pi)$ before fitting its parameters. A poor template constrains the analysis to unhelpful failure descriptions.

### Method 1: A Layered Explainability Workflow

These methods answer different questions and are strongest when used together:

1. **Visualize rollouts and policy slices.** Find broad behavior, discontinuities, unexpected regions, and candidate edge cases.
2. **Measure feature importance.** Use perturbation as an intuitive reference, gradients for efficiency, and integrated gradients when saturation matters.
3. **Test interactions.** Apply Shapley-style attribution when redundant or cooperating features make one-at-a-time sensitivity unreliable.
4. **Expose local or global logic.** Fit a sparse local linear model or a compact decision tree, and report its fidelity in the region being explained.
5. **Generate feasible counterfactuals.** Identify minimal, realistic changes that alter the outcome.
6. **Cluster failures.** Choose state, action, disturbance, or temporal-logic features that produce actionable failure modes.
7. **Validate the explanations.** Randomize models, perturb important features, compare methods, inspect surrogate error, and test whether counterfactuals remain within the system’s feasible domain.

The workflow is deliberately layered. Visualization may reveal a suspicious policy notch; local attribution can identify which variables control it; a surrogate can express the boundary as a rule; a counterfactual can show the smallest encounter change that crosses it; and failure clustering can determine whether the same mechanism appears across many simulations.

## 2. From Attribution to Causality for Explainable AI

When an AI system fails in production, accuracy scores and benchmark results are no longer enough. Engineers, executives, regulators, and users need answers to three concrete questions:

1. Why did the failure happen?
2. What can be changed to mitigate it?
3. How can we show that the specific failure mode has been addressed?

These questions give explainability a practical engineering purpose. An explanation should do more than make a model appear understandable: it should help locate a failure, guide a remedy, and support an assurance claim. The right method depends heavily on the system being studied. A controller with a two-dimensional state can often be inspected directly; an image classifier requires tools that identify influential regions; and a large language model may demand experiments on its internal representations.

Across these settings, explainability follows a broad progression—from observing associations at the input and output, to identifying and testing causal mechanisms inside the model.

### Explainability Begins With Failure Analysis

Safety-critical systems must be evaluated not only by their average performance but also by their worst behavior.

This makes explainability inseparable from debugging. A useful explanation should connect an observed failure to an actionable weakness such as missing training data, a spurious correlation, a brittle decision boundary, or an unsafe internal computation. Different techniques expose different parts of that chain, and no single technique is sufficient for every model or modality.

### Feature Attribution in Sequential Systems

Consider a Cart Pole controller that succeeds for many timesteps before the pole falls. Suppose noise entered the system at each timestep. Which noise event caused the failure?

#### Why leave-one-out analysis breaks down

A natural first experiment is leave-one-out ablation. Rerun the trajectory repeatedly, each time setting the noise at one timestep to zero. If removing the noise at timestep 25 prevents the fall, timestep 25 appears responsible.

This method is easy to understand, but it assumes features act independently. Imagine that three consecutive disturbances jointly push the system into an unrecoverable state. Removing any one of them may not prevent the failure, so each event appears unimportant even though the group is decisive. Redundant signals create the opposite problem: two features may encode the same information, allowing either one to conceal the importance of the other.

The underlying challenge is **credit assignment**—distributing responsibility among components that may interact, overlap, or compensate for one another.

#### Shapley values and coalition-based attribution

Shapley values address this problem by treating features as players in a cooperative game. Rather than measure the effect of removing one feature from the complete input, they measure that feature's marginal contribution across every possible coalition of other features.

As defined earlier, a feature's Shapley value is its weighted average marginal contribution across all coalitions that exclude it. The contribution to a particular coalition is the change in model output when that feature is added, and the combinatorial weighting averages this change over all possible feature orderings.

This formulation gives Shapley values an appealing fairness interpretation. Features that make identical contributions receive identical credit, irrelevant features receive none, and the individual attributions add up consistently to the total being explained. Because the method evaluates coalitions, it handles interactions and redundancy more systematically than a single leave-one-out test.

Its main weakness is cost. Exact evaluation requires an exponential number of subsets—or, equivalently, reasoning over a factorial number of orderings. Even a 40-timestep trajectory is too large for naive exact computation. Practical implementations therefore sample coalitions, approximate the expectation, or group related features such as adjacent timesteps.

There is also a deeper modeling choice: what does it mean for a feature to be “absent”? In a control trajectory, absence might mean zero noise. In a tabular model, it might mean substitution with a baseline or an expectation under some distribution. Different choices can produce different explanations, especially when features are correlated. A Shapley value is principled relative to the game that has been defined; it does not eliminate the need to define that game carefully.

### Direct Inspection for Low-Dimensional Models

Attribution is not always the best starting point. When a state space is small enough, direct visualization can reveal model behavior more clearly than a summary score.

For a controller with two state variables, engineers can query the policy across a grid and color each point by the selected action. The result exposes decision boundaries, discontinuities, and “dead zones” at a glance. This is particularly valuable for policies trained through behavioral cloning, in which a model learns to imitate an expert.

An expert controller may be so effective that it never visits dangerous states—for example, states where the pole is already tilted sharply. The cloned policy consequently receives no recovery examples from those regions. It performs well near the center of the training distribution, but runtime noise can push it into an uncovered edge where its actions become arbitrary. Visualization makes the root cause visible: the failure is not mysterious behavior inside the network, but missing data in a specific part of the state space.

Higher-dimensional systems make full visualization impossible, but two approximations remain useful:

- **Slicing** fixes some variables and plots the remaining two or three dimensions.
- **Dimensionality reduction** projects states into a lower-dimensional representation using methods such as PCA or t-SNE.

Both require caution. A slice can hide interactions with the fixed variables, while a projection can distort distances and decision boundaries. These plots are diagnostic views, not complete representations of the original space.

An even simpler technique generalizes across nearly every modality: sort validation examples by loss and inspect the worst cases. A pattern may become obvious—snowy scenes, rare lighting conditions, a particular demographic subgroup, or a repeated linguistic construction. Worst-case inspection does not provide a formal explanation, but it is often the fastest way to form a useful hypothesis about systematic failure.

### Explaining Vision Models

Images make direct state-space visualization infeasible. They also give high-capacity models countless opportunities to exploit irrelevant shortcuts. A classifier may associate a timestamp, a background, or a typical patch of sky with a label instead of recognizing the object itself. This is the **Clever Hans effect**: the model appears to solve the intended problem but actually relies on an unintended cue.

Vision explainability methods try to reveal which pixels or semantic regions drive a prediction.

#### Perturbation and masking

The most direct method is to cover patches of an image and measure the change in the model's output. If masking one region sharply reduces the target score, that region is treated as important.

Masking is intuitive and model-agnostic, but it requires many forward passes and inherits the limitations of leave-one-out reasoning. Several correlated regions may jointly encode the same feature, while an artificial black patch may itself push the image far outside the training distribution.

#### Gradient saliency and saturation

A faster alternative computes the gradient of the target output with respect to each input pixel. Large gradients identify pixels where a small local change would strongly affect the output.

Basic saliency maps are often noisy, and local gradients can be misleading when the model is saturated. If a classifier is extremely confident, its output may lie in a nearly flat region where the gradient is close to zero. Important pixels can then receive little saliency simply because the model has already accumulated overwhelming evidence by the final input.

#### Integrated gradients

Integrated gradients, introduced earlier, address saturation by accumulating gradients along a path from a baseline input—often a black image—to the actual input. Even if the gradient is almost zero at the final input, it may be large at intermediate points along the path. Integrating those gradients captures the accumulated influence that a single endpoint measurement misses.

The method also extends beyond images. Text tokens are discrete and cannot be faded in continuously, but their embedding vectors can be interpolated from a baseline embedding to the actual representation.

As with Shapley values, the baseline matters. A black image or zero embedding is convenient, but it may not represent a genuinely neutral input. The resulting attribution should therefore be interpreted in relation to the chosen reference.

#### Grad-CAM and semantic regions

Pixel-level maps can be too granular for human interpretation. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping, or Grad-CAM, instead computes importance with respect to late-stage feature maps in a convolutional network or vision model. These feature maps encode more abstract information, so the resulting heatmap can highlight a dog's head or another semantic region rather than a cloud of individual pixels.

Because late feature maps have lower spatial resolution than the original image, Grad-CAM heatmaps are upsampled and tend to look coarse or “blobby.” That loss of precision is part of the tradeoff: the explanation becomes easier to interpret conceptually, but less exact spatially.

#### Explanations need sanity checks

A visually persuasive heatmap is not automatically a faithful explanation. One critical test is model-parameter randomization. If the network's learned weights are progressively randomized, an explanation that genuinely depends on the model should change and eventually become noise. If the map remains nearly identical, the method may be recovering generic image structure such as edges rather than explaining the network's learned decision.

Other checks can perturb the features labeled important, compare against simple baselines, and test whether explanations remain stable under irrelevant input changes. Explainability methods should be evaluated as technical instruments, not judged by aesthetics alone.

### From Association to Mechanism

Feature attribution explains relationships between inputs and outputs. It does not necessarily reveal the computation a model performs internally, nor does it establish causality.

Consider an AI system used for credit scoring. A regulator prohibits the use of ethnicity, so engineers remove that field from the input. The system may nevertheless reconstruct a proxy from correlated variables such as location. An input-level explanation might show that location affects the score, but it cannot by itself prove which internal representation was formed or whether that representation causally controlled the decision.

This distinction mirrors the difference between observational and causal models of the world. Observational data can show that two variables occur together, but correlation alone cannot determine whether one causes the other or whether a third variable causes both. In causal notation, the conditional probability $P(Y \mid X)$ is fundamentally different from the interventional quantity $P(Y \mid do(X))$. The latter asks what would happen if $X$ were actively changed rather than merely observed.

The landscape can be organized along two dimensions:

| Explanation target | Association or correlation                       | Mechanism or cause                                    |
| :----------------- | :----------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------- |
| **The world**      | Observational and probabilistic models           | Causal graphs and physical laws                       |
| **The model**      | Saliency maps, perturbations, and Shapley values | Mechanistic interpretability and causal interventions |

Mechanistic interpretability occupies the model-and-mechanism quadrant. Its goal is to reverse-engineer the algorithms, representations, and circuits inside a neural network—the equivalent of discovering a causal graph or a set of physical laws for the model itself.

This is a stronger ambition than identifying an influential input. If researchers can find an internal representation associated with a protected attribute, disable it, and observe that the downstream behavior changes as predicted, they gain causal evidence about the model's computation. That evidence is much closer to what is needed for robust debugging and assurance.

### Opening the Black Box of Large Language Models

Large language models make mechanistic analysis difficult because their internal activations are dense, high-dimensional, and distributed. Researchers cannot generally identify one neuron for one human concept. The obstacle is **superposition**.

#### Superposition and distributed concepts

Neural networks can represent more concepts than they have dimensions by encoding concepts as directions in a high-dimensional space. Many nearly orthogonal directions can coexist in that space, allowing a single activation to contain a mixture of numerous features. Individual dimensions are therefore often polysemantic: they participate in several concepts rather than mapping cleanly to one.

This frustrates the intuitive strategy of inspecting neurons one by one. To reason about the model in human terms, researchers need a way to decompose dense activations into a larger set of sparse, interpretable features.

#### Sparse autoencoders as dictionary learning

Sparse autoencoders provide one approach. An autoencoder receives an internal LLM activation $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, maps it through an encoder to an overcomplete sparse representation $f \in \mathbb{R}^m$ where $m \gg d$, and then uses a decoder to reconstruct the original activation as $\hat{x}$. Training balances reconstruction fidelity against sparsity:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \|x - \hat{x}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|f\|_1
$$

The reconstruction term $\|x-\hat{x}\|_2^2$ preserves the information in the original activation. The L1 penalty $\lambda\|f\|_1$ encourages most learned features to remain inactive for any given input. The model must therefore reconstruct a dense activation using only a small number of entries from a much larger learned dictionary.

Ideally, the decoder's learned directions correspond to recognizable concepts, while $f$ reports which concepts are active in a particular context. Researchers can inspect the examples that activate each feature and assign tentative human interpretations to them.

Sparse autoencoders do not automatically solve interpretability. A learned feature may combine concepts, split one concept across several directions, or resist a clear label. Reconstruction quality and sparsity also compete: excessive sparsity discards information, while insufficient sparsity leaves the representation entangled. Nevertheless, SAEs provide candidate internal nodes on which causal experiments can be performed.

#### Intervention and circuit tracing

Once a candidate concept direction has been identified, researchers can alter its activation during a forward pass. Increasing a “Golden Gate Bridge” feature, for example, has been shown to make a model inject that concept into otherwise unrelated responses. The intervention changes behavior rather than merely correlating with it, providing evidence that the feature participates causally in generation.

The next step is **circuit tracing**: mapping how features across layers transmit and transform information from input to output. An ideal circuit-level explanation would identify the internal features that represent the relevant entity, relation, and answer, and show how their causal influence produces “Austin.”

This remains a formidable scaling problem. Large models may contain hundreds of thousands or millions of candidate features across many layers, with nonlinear and context-dependent interactions among them. Discovering a useful dictionary is only the beginning; mapping reliable circuits through that dictionary is a much larger challenge.

### Method 2: A Layered Workflow for Practical Explainability

The progression from visualization to circuit tracing suggests a pragmatic workflow rather than a competition for one universal method:

1. **Start with the failure.** Define the undesired behavior precisely and identify the worst-performing cases.
2. **Inspect the data and operating region.** Look for missing coverage, distribution shift, label problems, and recurring environmental patterns.
3. **Use the simplest suitable model-level probe.** Visualize low-dimensional policies, perturb inputs, or compute feature attributions.
4. **Validate the explanation.** Test sensitivity to baselines, randomize parameters, compare methods, and check whether changing important features changes the outcome.
5. **Intervene when causal confidence matters.** Manipulate inputs or internal activations and measure whether behavior changes as predicted.
6. **Retest the specific failure mode.** After mitigation, verify performance in the affected region and under related worst-case conditions.

This workflow maps directly to the three practical questions. Data inspection and attribution help explain why the failure occurred. Targeted collection, retraining, or circuit-level modification addresses what can be done. Controlled interventions and adversarial retesting provide evidence that the remedy affects the intended mechanism.

## 3. From Perception Errors to System Failures: Stress Testing Vision-Based Controllers

Machine learning has expanded what autonomous systems can perceive and control, but it has also changed the meaning of safety. A conventional controller can often be analyzed directly from its dynamics and decision rules. A vision-based controller, by contrast, may fail because of a runway marking, a change in lighting, an unexpected wall color, or another visual feature that had no obvious role in the system specification. Worse, a large perception error may be harmless after downstream compensation, while a small error at the wrong moment may cause a catastrophic system-level failure.

This mismatch makes component accuracy an inadequate safety metric. The important question is not simply whether a neural network makes a prediction error, but whether that error drives the closed-loop system into an unsafe state.

A useful response is to treat safety as a continuous process rather than a one-time certification exercise. Training-time constraints establish an initial safety baseline. Deployment-time monitoring detects situations in which that baseline may no longer apply. Systematic stress testing then searches for failure-inducing conditions, turns them into actionable data, and feeds them back into monitoring and retraining.

For vision-based controllers, Hamilton–Jacobi reachability provides a way to make the stress-testing stage mathematically precise. It identifies the initial states that lead to failure under the complete closed-loop system, then maps those states back to the visual inputs that produced the unsafe behavior. The result is more than a catalog of bad images: it is a system-level account of which visual errors actually matter.

### Why System Safety Is Different From Component Accuracy

An autonomy stack typically connects perception, prediction, planning, and control. Machine learning may appear in every layer, but conventional evaluation often isolates one component at a time. A perception network might be judged by classification accuracy, localization error, or the difference between its command and a reference command.

These metrics are useful, but they do not determine whether the physical system remains safe. Downstream planning and control may absorb a large upstream error. Conversely, a modest perception error can become critical when the system is close to a safety boundary, has little remaining control authority, or commits to a trajectory that cannot be corrected in time.

This distinction separates two kinds of stress testing:

- **Component-level testing** asks where an individual model is inaccurate or vulnerable.
- **System-level testing** asks where the resulting closed-loop trajectory reaches a defined failure set.

Random sampling is also insufficient. Safety-critical events are often rare, and high-dimensional visual spaces make exhaustive enumeration impossible. Stress testing must therefore be goal-directed: it should search backward from failure to discover the states—and ultimately the images—from which failure can occur.

### Turning a Vision Pipeline Into a Reachability Problem

Consider a dynamical system with state $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, control input $u$, and disturbance $d$. Its state evolves according to a dynamics model $f$. A visual observation function $I$ renders the image associated with the current state, and a learned controller $g$ maps that image to a control command. The complete closed-loop system is

$$
\dot{x} = f(x,\; g(I(x)),\; d)
$$

Here, $I: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$ may represent an RGB camera or simulator renderer, while $g: \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3} \to \mathcal{U}$ may be a CNN, ResNet, vision transformer, or another black-box controller.

The central simplification is to compose perception and control into an equivalent state-based policy $h(x) \triangleq g(I(x))$, giving the closed-loop dynamics $\dot{x}=f(x,h(x),d)$.

This composition does not remove perception from the real system. It creates a state-to-action representation that existing reachability tools can analyze. Once a failure region $\mathcal{F}$ has been defined in state space——the stress-testing target becomes a backward reachable tube (BRT):

```math
\text{BRT} = \left\{ x_0 \;\middle|\; \exists t \geq 0 : x(t; x_0, h) \in \mathcal{F} \right\}
```

The BRT contains the dangerous initial conditions: states whose trajectories eventually enter the failure region under the fixed controller. After computing it, the corresponding visual failures can be recovered by rendering the observations associated with those states.

This reduction is powerful, but it rests on important assumptions. The dynamics must be known or identified well enough for meaningful analysis, the failure set must be expressible in state space, and the observation function must be queryable—usually through a simulator. In the physical world, $I(x)$ is rarely determined by state alone. The same geometric state can produce different images under different lighting, weather, texture, or sensor conditions. A more complete model would include those environmental variables in the state or compute separate analyses across conditions.

### Hamilton–Jacobi Reachability as a Safety Game

Hamilton–Jacobi (HJ) reachability formalizes safety as a continuous-time adversarial game. The system dynamics are

$$
\dot{x} = f(x, u, d), \quad x \in \mathbb{R}^n, \; u \in \mathcal{U}, \; d \in \mathcal{D}
$$

The controller selects $u$ to preserve safety, while the disturbance $d$ represents both external forces and model uncertainty and acts adversarially. Safety is encoded by a continuous function $L$ whose sign identifies the failure region:

$$
L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}, \quad L(x) \begin{cases} < 0 & \text{if } x \in \mathcal{F} \text{ (failure)} \\ \geq 0 & \text{if } x \notin \mathcal{F} \text{ (safe)} \end{cases}
$$

A standard choice is the signed distance from the failure boundary, positive on the safe side and negative inside the failure region.

The safety score of an entire trajectory is its worst value over the time horizon $[0,T]$:

$$
J(x_0, u(\cdot), d(\cdot)) = \min_{t \in [0, T]} L(x(t; x_0, u, d))
$$

If the trajectory enters the failure set even once, $J$ becomes negative. The value function then asks how large a safety margin the best controller can preserve against the worst disturbance:

$$
V(x_0) = \max_{u(\cdot) \in \mathcal{U}} \min_{d(\cdot) \in \mathcal{D}} \; J(x_0, u(\cdot), d(\cdot))
$$

Its sign partitions the state space. When $V(x_0)<0$, the initial state belongs to the BRT: even the optimal safety controller cannot prevent the worst-case trajectory from reaching failure. When $V(x_0)\geq0$, some admissible control policy can keep the system safe over the modeled horizon.

In continuous time, dynamic programming yields the Hamilton–Jacobi–Isaacs partial differential equation:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}(x, t) + \min\left(0,\; \max_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \min_{d \in \mathcal{D}} \; \nabla_x V(x, t)^\top f(x, u, d)\right) = 0
$$

with terminal condition $V(x, 0) = L(x)$. The inner product $\nabla_x V^\top f$ is the rate at which the dynamics change the safety value. The controller maximizes that rate while the disturbance minimizes it. The outer $\min(0,\cdot)$ preserves the “reach failure at any time” interpretation by ensuring that the unsafe set can grow backward through time rather than lose states already classified as unsafe.

The same dynamic-programming structure has a discrete-time Bellman form. Once $V$ is available, its gradient can also define an optimal safety controller that moves the system toward a higher safety value against the worst modeled disturbance. This captures physical asymmetries that geometric margins miss. HJ reachability reflects that difference through the dynamics rather than through an arbitrary fixed distance threshold.

The cost of this global analysis is computational. Classical HJ solvers discretize the full state space, so their cost grows exponentially with state dimension. Exact grid-based methods are generally practical only for relatively low-dimensional systems—often around six state variables or fewer. Higher-dimensional applications require decomposition, abstraction, neural approximations, or other data-driven surrogates, each of which weakens or changes the original guarantee.

### Stress Testing a Fixed Vision-Based Controller

In controller synthesis, HJ reachability optimizes over $u$. Stress testing has a different goal: the vision-based controller already exists, and the task is to characterize its failures. Substituting $h(x)=g(I(x))$ removes the maximization over control:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}(x,t) + \min\left(0,\; \min_{d \in \mathcal{D}} \nabla_x V(x,t)^\top f(x,\; h(x),\; d)\right) = 0
$$

with terminal condition $V(x, 0) = L(x)$. The finite-horizon BRT is then

```math
\text{BRT}_T = \left\{ x_0 \;\middle|\; V(x_0, T) < 0 \right\}
```

The corresponding failure-image set is

```math
\mathcal{I}_{\text{fail}} = \left\{ I(x) \;\middle|\; x \in \text{BRT}_T \right\}
```

This procedure produces a concrete workflow:

1. Define the physical dynamics, admissible disturbances, time horizon, and failure set.
2. Query the simulator and controller to evaluate $h(x)=g(I(x))$ throughout the state grid.
3. Solve the fixed-controller HJ equation to obtain $V$ and threshold $V<0$ to extract the BRT.
4. Render the states in the BRT to obtain safety-critical images.
5. Inspect, cluster, or label those images to understand recurring failure patterns.

The crucial result is that component error and system failure are not interchangeable. Let $e(x)=\|g(I(x))-g^*(x)\|$ measure the controller’s prediction error against a reference action. A high-error command may be corrected later, while a low-error command can still push the system across an irreversible boundary. Reachability filters perception mistakes by their physical consequences and focuses engineering attention on the subset that changes the safety outcome.

### Turning Discovered Failures Into Runtime Protection

The failure-image set can support a second layer of defense. Given BRT-derived failure images $I_{fail}$ and safe images $I_{safe}$, a binary classifier can learn to distinguish the two classes. At runtime, it applies a threshold to its predicted failure probability:

$$
\text{AnomalyDetector}(I) = \begin{cases} \text{FAILURE} & \text{if } p_\theta(y=\text{fail} \mid I) > \tau \\ \text{SAFE} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

If an image is flagged, the system can slow down, stop, switch to a model-based backup controller, or request human intervention. The threshold $\tau$ expresses an operational trade-off: lowering it reduces missed failures but increases false alarms and fallback activations.

This monitor should not be mistaken for a general out-of-distribution detector. It recognizes failure patterns represented by the environments and conditions used during stress testing. A detector trained on ordinary runway conditions has no automatic guarantee on a snowy runway, a novel sensor artifact, or a visually unfamiliar building. Its protection is conditional on the coverage of the failure dataset.

### Retraining Does Not Guarantee Monotonic Safety

The same failure set can be used to repair the controller. Failure images are paired with corrected actions and added to the original training set.

After retraining, the BRT is computed again. A smaller tube in the targeted region shows that the controller has learned to handle the discovered failure mode.

Yet the updated controller may also develop failures in states that were previously safe. Neural-network training can redistribute errors, overfit the new examples, or forget behavior learned from the original distribution. Formally, adding relevant data does not imply that the new BRT is a subset of the old one:

$$
\mathcal{D}_{\text{retrain}} \supset \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} \not\Rightarrow \text{BRT}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{retrain}}) \subseteq \text{BRT}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}})
$$

This non-monotonicity changes the engineering process. Retraining cannot be treated as the final step in remediation; it must be followed by a complete re-evaluation of the controller, including regions that were safe before the update. Continual-learning tools such as replay, parameter regularization, and constrained optimization may reduce regression, but they do not by themselves establish a system-level monotonic safety guarantee.

### Generalization and the Limits of Offline Stress Testing

Reachability results are tied to the modeled environment. If visual conditions change, the observation function changes, the learned controller’s actions may change, and the BRT may change with them.

Offline stress testing offers fast deployment-time monitoring because the expensive reachability computation is performed in advance. Its weakness is coverage: every unmodeled environment creates a gap between the analyzed system and the deployed one. Online stress testing could adapt the analysis as conditions change, but solving reachability problems continuously is computationally demanding.

Digital twins offer a possible middle ground. Neural radiance fields and Gaussian splatting can reconstruct visual environments and render novel viewpoints, potentially making it easier to evaluate $I(x)$ in a new location. They solve only part of the problem, however. A visual reconstruction does not supply the system dynamics, disturbance bounds, semantic safety constraints, or proof that the reconstructed observations match the deployed sensor closely enough for safety analysis.

Interpretability creates another bottleneck. BRT analysis identifies failure states and images, not causal explanations. Engineers may still need to mask image regions, compare controlled variations, and inspect trajectories manually to determine whether a controller relied on a marking, color, shadow, or object. Foundation models may help organize and describe failure images, but automated semantic attribution remains a hypothesis to validate rather than a formal capability.

### Modular Pipelines Need System-Level Risk Measures

System-level Perception-Aware Risk Quantification, or SPARQ, addresses this problem with a learned surrogate. It consumes perception uncertainty $m$, an ego plan $\pi_{\text{ego}}$, and scene context $s$, then predicts one of three risk levels:

$$
\text{SPARQ}_\theta\left(\underbrace{m}_{\text{perception uncertainty}},\; \underbrace{\pi_{\text{ego}}}_{\text{ego plan}},\; \underbrace{s}_{\text{scene}}\right) \to \{0, 1, 2\}
$$

The outputs correspond to SAFE, RISKY, and CRITICAL, and the model can optionally propose a repaired plan. Its inputs may include ego and agent histories, a road graph, the candidate trajectory, and the perception monitor’s missing-agent distribution.

Training labels come from expensive offline evaluation. For a range of scenarios and miss patterns, agents are removed or perturbed in simulation, the full prediction and planning pipeline is rerun, and the resulting plan is labeled by its safety outcome. This moves the expensive system-level reasoning offline. The trained network then approximates the result quickly enough for deployment—about 24 ms at 42 Hz in the cited setup, compared with 100 ms for the original 10 Hz safety evaluation. The joint context matters: a missed agent is safe if the ego plan avoids it, but critical if the plan intersects its predicted trajectory.

SPARQ illustrates a practical compromise between exhaustive safety evaluation and real-time latency. It also introduces a crucial qualification: a learned surrogate predicts the result of the safety analysis; it is not itself a formal safety certificate. Its reliability depends on the coverage and fidelity of the offline scenario distribution, and its behavior on out-of-distribution scenes remains uncertain.

Modular and end-to-end systems therefore present different assurance problems. End-to-end controllers make it easy to state an overall failure criterion such as collision, but they make root-cause attribution difficult. Modular systems expose intermediate representations and permit targeted repair, but uncertainty must be propagated through several interacting components. The desired combination—system-level safety criteria with component-level attribution—remains an open research problem.

### The Remaining Research Frontier

The mathematical mismatch between component error and system risk can be stated as a set relationship. For an error threshold $\epsilon>0$,

$$
\{x : e(x) > \epsilon\} \not\supseteq \text{BRT}_T \quad \text{and} \quad \{x : e(x) > \epsilon\} \not\subseteq \text{BRT}_T
$$

The high-error set is neither a superset nor a subset of the system-level failure set. Component testing therefore produces both false priorities—large errors that do not affect safety—and blind spots—small errors that do.

Uncertainty propagation is another unresolved challenge. A modular pipeline should ideally preserve a distribution over possible states rather than collapse perception into a single point estimate. A particle filter provides one conceptual approximation:

$$
p(x_t \mid z_{1:t}) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \delta_{x_t^{(i)}}
$$

The particles $x_t^{(i)}$ represent possible states conditioned on the observation history $z_{1:t}$. Propagating enough particles through nonlinear learned prediction and planning modules can become expensive, but discarding the distribution hides exactly the low-probability, high-consequence possibilities that safety analysis should retain.

Scenario generation presents a complementary opportunity. Accident reports and natural-language descriptions could be converted into simulation scenarios to broaden stress-test coverage and expose rare conditions. Generative models may accelerate this process, but text-to-simulation is not a one-to-one mapping: the generated geometry, timing, behavior, and sensor conditions must still be checked against the source event and the intended safety claim.

## 4. Beyond Offline Validation: : Runtime Monitoring for Safety-Critical AI

No validation model can contain the entire world. A system may perform well across simulation, falsification, probability estimation, formal verification, and explainability studies, yet still encounter a situation its designers never anticipated. These are not merely rare test cases. They expose a permanent gap between the conditions represented during validation and the open-ended conditions of deployment.

Runtime monitoring addresses that gap by evaluating safety continuously while the system operates. It does not replace offline validation. Instead, it detects when offline evidence may no longer apply, when a model is too uncertain to trust, or when the system is approaching a failure region already identified during analysis. A monitor can then trigger a fallback such as stopping, switching to a backup controller, entering a safe mode, or transferring control to a human operator.

The resulting safety architecture follows the Swiss cheese model: every defense has holes, but several defenses aimed at different failure mechanisms make it less likely that one hazardous event will pass through the entire system. In practical terms, a runtime monitor asks four questions:

1. Is the current state inside the operational design domain that was validated?
2. Is the prediction reliable despite unavoidable noise or randomness?
3. Does the model have enough knowledge to justify its prediction here?
4. Is the system entering a state already known to have a high probability of failure?

Operationally, the system continues with its primary controller only while the runtime state remains inside the ODD, uncertainty is low, and the state is outside known failure regions; otherwise, it triggers a fallback. Each condition protects against a different kind of uncertainty rather than forcing one detector to solve every safety problem.

### The Boundary of Offline Evidence

Offline validation always relies on a model: a simulator, a probability distribution, a formal abstraction, a dataset, or some combination of them. The result is therefore conditional. A proof applies under its assumptions; an estimated failure rate applies to its sampling distribution; and a test result applies to the conditions represented in the test.

The operational design domain makes that conditionality explicit. It is the set of states, environments, and operating conditions for which a system was designed and validated. It may be described manually or inferred from the validation data. Leaving the ODD does not prove that the system will fail, but it does invalidate the inference that previous validation evidence still supports safe operation.

This distinction matters because runtime monitoring is not expected to recognize every possible edge case by name. A list-based detector would immediately fail on the next novel event. The monitor instead needs to determine whether the current observation remains sufficiently similar to the validated region or whether the system has crossed the boundary of its available evidence.

### Monitoring the Operational Design Domain

A data-driven ODD treats the validation dataset as a representation of the region in which the system has been evaluated. Several techniques can turn that dataset into a runtime membership test. They differ in memory cost, geometric flexibility, and dependence on additional assumptions.

#### Nearest-neighbor monitoring

The most direct method accepts a runtime point when enough validation examples lie nearby:

```math
\text{ODD}_{\text{NN}}(\epsilon, k) = \left\{ x \;\middle|\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\left[\|x - x_i\| \leq \epsilon\right] \geq k \right\}
```

The query $x$ is in the ODD if at least $k$ of the $N$ validation points $x_i$ fall within distance $\epsilon$. Setting $k=1$ yields a simple nearest-neighbor rule. Requiring more neighbors prevents isolated validation outliers from enlarging the accepted region.

This method is transparent and directly grounded in the data, but it requires the runtime system to retain the reference set. A KD-tree can reduce a typical query to $O(\log N)$ time, yet memory remains $O(N \cdot d)$ for $N$ points of dimension $d$. Clustering offers an approximation: replace the complete dataset with $K$ representative centers, reducing storage to $O(K \cdot d)$ when $K \ll N$.

<details><summary>The following conceptual implementation preserves the full validation set in a KD-tree:</summary>

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors

class NearestNeighborODDMonitor:
    def __init__(self, data, epsilon, k=1):
        # data: (N, d) array of validation data points
        self.epsilon = epsilon
        self.k = k
        self.nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=k, algorithm='kd_tree')
        self.nn.fit(data)  # O(N log N) preprocessing, O(N*d) memory

    def is_in_ODD(self, x):
        # x: (d,) query point
        distances, _ = self.nn.kneighbors([x])
        # Check if all k neighbors are within epsilon
        return np.all(distances[0] <= self.epsilon)
```

</details>

The implementation also reveals the central trade-off: fast lookup does not eliminate the cost of carrying validation data onboard.

#### Convex-hull monitoring

A more compact representation encloses the validation points in a convex hull. A single hull is often too permissive for a non-convex dataset because it includes the empty regions between separate modes. Clustering the data first and taking a union of hulls produces a more flexible boundary:

$$
\text{ODD}_{\text{hull}} = \bigcup_{j=1}^{K} \text{ConvexHull}(C_j)
$$

where $\{C_1, \ldots, C_K\}$ is a $K$-means clustering of the validation data.

Each cluster $C_j$ contributes a polytope. A point belongs to its convex hull precisely when it can be written as a convex combination of the cluster points. Equivalently, membership holds when the linear program $\min \mathbf{0}^\top \lambda$ subject to $\sum_i \lambda_i x_i = x$, $\lambda_i \geq 0$, and $\sum_i \lambda_i = 1$ is feasible.

More clusters fit a non-convex domain more accurately but increase runtime storage and checking cost. In the limit of one cluster per point, the representation approaches a nearest-neighbor-style description. With too few clusters, it may accept states that lie between valid regions but were never themselves validated.

#### Distributional level sets

Instead of constructing a geometric enclosure, a monitor can fit a probability density $p(x)$ to the validation data and accept its high-density region:

```math
\text{ODD}_{\text{dist}}(\tau) = \left\{ x \;\middle|\; p(x) \geq \tau \right\}
```

The threshold $\tau$ determines how much of the fitted distribution belongs to the ODD. A higher value produces a smaller, more conservative domain; a lower value increases coverage but risks accepting weakly supported states.

A single multivariate Gaussian rarely captures a complicated, multimodal operating domain. A Gaussian mixture model is more expressive:

$$
p(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j \, \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_j, \Sigma_j)
$$

Each component has weight $\pi_j$, mean $\mu_j$, and covariance $\Sigma_j$, with $\pi_j \geq 0$ and $\sum_j \pi_j = 1$. Its density is

$$
\mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right)
$$

Mixtures can model separated modes and curved, non-convex high-density regions without explicitly constructing many polytopes. The quality of the resulting ODD still depends on the chosen model family, the number of components, the validation data, and the threshold.

#### Learned ODD classifiers

A binary classifier can learn an even more flexible boundary:

```math
\text{ODD}_{\text{clf}}(\tau) = \left\{ x \;\middle|\; p_\theta(\text{in-ODD} \mid x) \geq \tau \right\}
```

The network estimates the probability that $x$ belongs to the ODD, and $\tau$ trades false alarms against missed detections. The method can represent complex nonlinear boundaries, but it introduces a serious data problem: binary training requires examples from both inside and outside the ODD. Out-of-ODD data is precisely what designers often do not possess. If the unknown environment were already well represented, the system could potentially have been designed and validated to handle it.

That limitation makes the classifier powerful only when its negative-data generation process is credible. Otherwise, it distinguishes the ODD from a selected collection of known alternatives rather than from the genuinely open world.

### High-Dimensional Inputs and Feature Collapse

All four ODD methods become harder to apply as input dimension grows. Even a $64\times64$ grayscale image contains 4,096 pixel dimensions. In such spaces, finite datasets cover only a tiny fraction of the possible volume, distance measures become less informative, and data requirements grow rapidly. This is the curse of dimensionality.

The standard response is to exploit the manifold hypothesis: although an image is represented by thousands or millions of numbers, the meaningful images for a task may lie near a much lower-dimensional structure. An encoder $\phi$ can map the original input $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ into a latent vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$, where $m \ll d$, after which any ODD monitor can operate in latent space:

```math
\text{ODD}_{\text{latent}}(\tau) = \left\{ x \;\middle|\; \text{ODD\_Monitor}(\phi(x)) \geq \tau \right\}
```

For an autoencoder, the encoder is trained together with a decoder by minimizing reconstruction error:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AE}} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \|x - \text{Dec}(\phi(x))\|^2 \right]
$$

This compression makes distance-based monitoring more tractable, but it creates a dangerous failure mode: feature collapse. The encoder is optimized to reconstruct the data on which it was trained, not to preserve separability for every unknown input. Distinct out-of-distribution images may therefore map into the same latent region as validated images. Nighttime scenes, for example, could collapse onto codes occupied by daytime scenes and pass the ODD monitor even though the original observations are operationally different.

There is no general guarantee that dimensionality reduction maps an unknown input outside the learned ODD. Latent-space monitoring should therefore be treated as another imperfect layer, not as proof that an observation is familiar.

### Two Different Meanings of Uncertainty

Passing an ODD check is necessary but insufficient. A system may be operating under familiar conditions and still face an ambiguous observation, a noisy sensor, or an intrinsically unpredictable event. Conversely, it may produce a confident prediction in a region where it has little supporting data.

These cases correspond to two distinct kinds of uncertainty:

- **Output, or aleatoric, uncertainty** comes from irreducible randomness in the environment or data-generating process. More data can describe that randomness more accurately, but it cannot remove it.
- **Model, or epistemic, uncertainty** comes from insufficient knowledge. It is reducible in principle because collecting representative data in the uncertain region can constrain the model.

The distinction is sometimes summarized as “I know that I do not know” versus “I do not even know what this is.” A model may understand that a blurry observation supports several plausible labels; that is output uncertainty. Presented with an object or situation outside its learned concepts, it may not have a meaningful basis for any prediction; that is model uncertainty.

A safety system needs both measurements because one cannot substitute for the other. An aleatoric estimator learned from data cannot be trusted automatically where no data exists, and a model-disagreement measure does not by itself describe randomness that all well-informed models should recognize.

### Quantifying Output Uncertainty

For continuous predictions, a conventional regression model outputs a point estimate. A heteroscedastic model instead predicts both a mean $f_\theta(x)$ and an input-dependent variance $\sigma_\theta^2(x)$. Under a Gaussian observation model, training minimizes the negative log-likelihood

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ \frac{(y_i - f_\theta(x_i))^2}{\sigma_\theta^2(x_i)} + \log(\sigma_\theta^2(x_i)) \right]
$$

The two terms constrain each other. The squared-error term encourages the mean to fit the observations, but an example contributes less when the model predicts high variance. The logarithmic term prevents the network from explaining every error by inflating its variance without limit. The result is an input-dependent estimate of aleatoric uncertainty: noisy or ambiguous regions can receive larger variances than consistent regions.

The loss is elegant but can be difficult to optimize. Predicted variance may collapse toward zero or grow excessively without suitable parameterization and initialization. More fundamentally, the variance target is learned from observed data. A network can therefore report unjustified confidence outside the region represented during training.

#### Classification probabilities require calibration

A classifier already emits a distribution when it uses a softmax layer. A nearly uniform distribution suggests ambiguity, while a sharply peaked distribution suggests confidence. Modern neural networks, however, are often overconfident: the softmax score may not match the empirical probability that a prediction is correct.

Temperature scaling provides a simple post-training correction:

$$
p(y=c \mid x) = \frac{\exp(\lambda \cdot z_c)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp(\lambda \cdot z_j)}
$$

where $\lambda = 1/T$.

Here, $z_c$ is the logit for class $c$, $K$ is the number of classes, and the scalar $\lambda$ is selected on a held-out calibration dataset by minimizing negative log-likelihood. When $\lambda=1$, the probabilities are unchanged. As $\lambda$ approaches zero, the distribution becomes more uniform; as it grows, the distribution approaches an absolute argmax decision. Because the same positive scalar multiplies every logit, temperature scaling changes confidence without changing the predicted class.

Calibration gives probabilistic outputs an operational meaning. If predictions reported at 80 percent confidence are correct approximately 80 percent of the time on representative data, thresholds can be connected more credibly to fallback policies. That statement remains conditional on the calibration distribution matching deployment.

#### Prediction sets and conformal prediction

Sometimes a set is more actionable than a probability vector. A prediction set returns several possible outputs and is designed to contain the correct answer with a target frequency such as 95 percent. A small set signals a decisive prediction; a large set makes uncertainty visible without pretending that one label is reliable.

Conformal prediction constructs such sets with finite-sample coverage guarantees under its exchangeability conditions, even when the underlying model is not probabilistically calibrated. It does not make the base predictor more accurate, and it does not eliminate distribution shift. It supplies a principled wrapper that converts model scores into prediction sets whose coverage can be evaluated directly.

### Quantifying Model Uncertainty

Aleatoric methods cannot solve epistemic uncertainty because they learn their uncertainty behavior from the same data that constrain the predictor. In a data-sparse region, the network has no target variance from which to learn. It may therefore extrapolate with confidence despite having no evidential basis for doing so.

Bayesian inference offers the principled response. Rather than selecting one parameter vector $\theta$, it maintains a posterior distribution over models that are plausible after observing dataset $D$. Prediction averages over that posterior:

$$
P(y \mid x, D) = \int_{\theta} P(y \mid x, \theta) \cdot P(\theta \mid D) \, d\theta
$$

In regions supported by data, plausible models tend to agree, yielding low epistemic uncertainty. Where evidence is sparse, models that fit the observed data can extrapolate differently, and their disagreement exposes the missing knowledge.

For a deep neural network, the integral over parameter space is intractable. Markov chain Monte Carlo can approximate difficult posterior distributions, but it is usually too expensive at modern network scale. Deep ensembles provide a practical approximation:

$$
P(y \mid x, D) \approx \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} P(y \mid x, \theta_m)
$$

where $\theta_m \sim P(\theta \mid D)$ (or practically, $\theta_m$ are models trained from different random initializations to high likelihood).

Each of the $M$ networks starts from a different random initialization and is trained independently. At runtime, their predictions are averaged, while disagreement between members acts as a proxy for epistemic uncertainty. If each member also predicts a mean and variance, the average of the member variances captures aleatoric uncertainty, while the variance of the member means captures epistemic uncertainty.

Deep ensembles scale more readily than exact Bayesian inference and can be trained in parallel, but they are still an approximation. Their safety value depends on diversity. If every member converges to a functionally similar solution or shares the same out-of-distribution behavior, the ensemble may be unanimously and confidently wrong. Random initialization encourages diversity; it does not guarantee it.

Gaussian processes illustrate the desired epistemic behavior cleanly because their predictive variance naturally grows away from observed data. Their computational and high-dimensional scaling limitations make them difficult to use directly for large image-based systems, which is one reason deep ensembles remain attractive despite their weaker connection to exact Bayesian averaging.

### Monitoring Known Failure Regions

Even a state that is inside the ODD and produces a low-uncertainty prediction can be dangerous. Familiarity and confidence do not imply safety.

Offline validation methods such as probabilistic reachability analysis and Monte Carlo rollout can identify states with high failure probability. Failure monitoring brings that information into deployment. There are two main approaches:

1. Run the original safety analysis online from the current state. This gives the analysis access to current conditions but is often too computationally expensive for a high-frequency control loop.
2. Precompute a map or lookup table of failure probabilities offline. At runtime, check whether the current state or predicted trajectory enters a high-risk region and trigger a fallback when the stored risk exceeds a safety threshold.

The second approach shifts expensive computation out of the control loop. A compact surrogate can also approximate an offline reachability or rollout result when the original map is too large. The compromise is familiar: a lookup table or learned surrogate is fast, but its reliability depends on offline coverage and approximation quality.

Test-time augmentation supplies another practical signal, especially in computer vision. The system creates several perturbed versions of the current image and passes them through the same network. If a detection or bounding box disappears under small changes, the output is sensitive and therefore less trustworthy. This procedure acts as a proxy for uncertainty, although running tens or hundreds of inferences per frame imposes a significant computational cost.

### The Complete Runtime Safety Pipeline

The monitoring layers can be assembled into one decision process:

1. **Check ODD:** Is $x_t \in \text{ODD}_{\text{train}}$?
   - _If out-of-ODD, trigger fallback._
2. **Check Model Uncertainty:** Is $\text{Var}_{\text{ensemble}}(f(x_t))$ high?
   - _If epistemic uncertainty high, trigger fallback._
3. **Check Output Uncertainty:** Is $\sigma^2(x_t)$ high, or is the prediction set large?
   - _If aleatoric uncertainty too high for task limits, trigger fallback._
4. **Check Failure Regions:** Is $P(\text{failure} \mid x_t) > \tau_{\text{safe}}$?
   - _If state is known to be dangerous, trigger fallback._
5. **Proceed:** If all checks pass, apply primary control $u_t = f(x_t)$.

In a deployed implementation, these checks become independent monitor modules, each with thresholds tied to operational risk. The ODD test detects novel environments. Ensemble disagreement detects missing knowledge. Predicted variance, calibrated probabilities, or prediction-set size expose unavoidable ambiguity. Failure-region monitoring recognizes known limits of the controller.

Independence between these checks is valuable. If every monitor relies on the same representation, dataset, or model architecture, a shared failure mode can defeat them simultaneously. Defense in depth is strongest when different layers use different evidence and fail for different reasons.

The response to a flag must also be designed, validated, and reachable in time. A monitor that detects danger without a feasible fallback does not make the system safe. Human takeover is useful only if the operator receives enough warning and situational context. Safe-mode design is therefore part of runtime assurance, not an action to specify after monitoring is complete.

### Thresholds, Trade-Offs, and Residual Risk

Every monitoring approach contains thresholds: $\epsilon$ and $k$ for nearest neighbors, $\tau$ for density and classifier boundaries, calibration targets for predictive probabilities, disagreement limits for ensembles, and a tolerated probability of failure. These values determine the balance between false positives and false negatives.

A conservative monitor triggers fallback frequently. That may be acceptable when fallback is cheap and benign, but excessive alerts can reduce system availability, encourage operators to ignore warnings, or create new risks through unnecessary mode switching. A permissive monitor preserves availability but allows more hazardous states to pass. Threshold selection is therefore an application-level safety decision, not a generic machine-learning hyperparameter choice.

The same caution applies to representation quality. An ODD is only as reliable as the validation data that defines it. A probability density is only as suitable as its model assumptions. An ensemble is only as informative as its member diversity. A precomputed failure map is only as valid as the dynamics, disturbances, and environments included in the offline analysis.

Most importantly, being inside the ODD is not a safety guarantee. It means the current state resembles the region in which evidence was collected. Low uncertainty is not a safety guarantee either; a model can be certain about a dangerous trajectory. Runtime assurance emerges from the combination of complementary evidence and a validated fallback, while residual risk remains whenever assumptions, data, or models fail together.
