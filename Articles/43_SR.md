# Automated Safety Analysis: From Rare-Event Sampling to Reachability Guarantees

Safety validation has two complementary sides. Sampling-based methods try to find failures and estimate how often they occur under the nominal operating distribution. Reachability methods ask a stronger set-based question: given bounded assumptions about initial states, disturbances, and dynamics, which states can the system possibly reach?

Safety analysis becomes useful when it says exactly what has been established. Direct Monte Carlo can reveal common failures, but rare failures demand variance-reduction tools such as importance sampling, cross-entropy updates, multiple proposals, population methods, and sequential Monte Carlo. MCMC and smoothing help discover the geometry of the failure region, while likelihood ratios turn biased sampling back into nominal probability estimates.

Reachability provides the complementary guarantee-oriented view. Linear systems expose a clean set-propagation algebra based on linear transformations and Minkowski sums. Nonlinear systems require support functions, interval arithmetic, mean-value or Taylor inclusion functions, and controlled overapproximation. Discrete abstractions then turn continuous safety questions into graph search, invariant detection, and probabilistic dynamic programming.

Across all of these methods, the central trade-off is the same: make the computation focused enough to be tractable, but conservative or correctly weighted enough that the result still answers the original safety question.

## 1. Importance Sampling for Rare-Event Failure Probability Estimation

Estimating the probability of rare failures is one of the central problems in simulation-based safety analysis. A stochastic system may fail only once in millions or billions of nominal rollouts, but that small probability can still matter enormously in safety-critical settings. Direct Monte Carlo gives a simple baseline, yet it quickly becomes impractical when failures are rare. Importance sampling addresses this problem by deliberately sampling trajectories from more failure-relevant distributions while correcting the estimate with likelihood ratios.

This explains the main ideas behind importance sampling for failure probability estimation, starting from the basic estimator and moving through practical proposal-design methods: cross-entropy, adaptive cross-entropy, multiple importance sampling, population Monte Carlo, and sequential Monte Carlo. The common thread is simple: every method tries to approximate the ideal failure distribution without already knowing the failure probability.

### The Rare-Event Estimation Problem

Consider a stochastic dynamical system whose randomness comes from an initial state and a sequence of disturbances. A complete rollout is represented as a trajectory $\tau$. The nominal trajectory distribution $p(\tau)$ is the distribution induced by the system model under ordinary operating conditions.

The direct Monte Carlo estimator samples trajectories from the nominal model and counts failures:

$$
\hat{p}_{\text{fail}}^{\text{direct}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}[\tau_i \text{ is a failure}], \quad \tau_i \sim p(\tau)
$$

This estimator is conceptually clean and unbiased. Its problem is not bias; its problem is sample efficiency. If failures are extremely rare, most finite sample sets contain no failures at all, producing an estimate of zero. Even when failures are observed, fixed relative accuracy requires a sample size that grows inversely with the failure probability.

The absolute variance becomes small as $p_{\text{fail}}$ becomes small, but the relative error becomes large because useful accuracy still depends on seeing enough failures. In practical terms, a direct estimator can require an astronomical number of rollouts before it produces a useful estimate.

### Importance Sampling: Bias the Samples, Correct the Estimate

Importance sampling replaces nominal sampling with sampling from a proposal distribution $q(\tau)$. The proposal should place more probability mass near failure-relevant trajectories. Because samples no longer come from the nominal model, each sample receives an importance weight:

$$
\hat{p}_{\text{fail}}^{\text{IS}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \mathbb{1}[\tau_i \text{ is a failure}], \quad \tau_i \sim q(\tau)
$$

$$
w_i = \frac{p(\tau_i)}{q(\tau_i)}
$$

The derivation is the standard change-of-measure identity:

$$
p_{\text{fail}} = \int \mathbb{1}[\text{failure}] \, p(\tau)\, d\tau = \int \mathbb{1}[\text{failure}] \frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)} \, q(\tau)\, d\tau = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim q}\left[\mathbb{1}[\text{failure}] \cdot \frac{p(\tau)}{q(\tau)}\right]
$$

This identity explains both the power and the danger of importance sampling. A good proposal can dramatically reduce variance. A poor proposal can produce unstable weights and perform worse than direct Monte Carlo.

The nominal density is usually available from the system model. For a factorized trajectory model, sampling rollouts gives trajectories, and evaluating the factors gives the numerator needed for importance weights.

### The Support Constraint

Importance sampling only works if the proposal can sample every failure trajectory that has nonzero nominal probability. Formally:

$$
q(\tau) > 0 \quad \forall\, \tau \text{ such that } p(\tau)\,\mathbb{1}[\text{failure}] > 0
$$

If a proposal assigns zero density to part of the failure region, no amount of reweighting can recover the missing probability mass. The estimator becomes biased because some possible failures are unreachable.

This requirement matters more for probability estimation than for falsification. If the goal is merely to find a failure, it may be acceptable to ignore unlikely failure regions and focus on the most likely one. If the goal is to estimate the total failure probability, every nonzero-probability failure mode must remain in support.

A Gaussian proposal often satisfies this condition automatically in continuous Euclidean spaces because it has full support. A bounded uniform proposal can fail the condition if possible failures lie outside its interval, making the resulting probability estimate biased.

### The Ideal Proposal and Why It Is Unavailable

The best possible importance sampling proposal is the conditional distribution of trajectories given failure. This is the nominal trajectory distribution restricted to the failure region and renormalized:

$$
q^*(\tau) = p(\tau \mid \text{failure}) = \frac{p(\tau)\, \mathbb{1}[\text{failure}]}{p_{\text{fail}}}
$$

The reason this proposal is ideal is direct. Under $q^*$, every sampled trajectory is a failure, and for every failing trajectory the likelihood ratio is the same constant, $p_{\text{fail}}$. The estimator therefore returns $p_{\text{fail}}$ exactly for every sample set.

This is a beautiful but impractical result. The normalizing constant of the failure distribution is exactly $p_{\text{fail}}$, the unknown quantity we are trying to estimate. Practical importance sampling methods are therefore attempts to approximate $q^*$ with a tractable proposal.

### Fitting a Proposal from Failure Samples

One direct strategy is to obtain samples from the failure distribution and fit a parametric proposal, such as a Gaussian. If failure samples can be drawn by MCMC or rejection sampling, the fitted proposal can be estimated by maximum likelihood.

This turns an intractable optimal proposal into a tractable approximation $q_\theta$. The challenge is that obtaining representative failure samples is itself difficult, especially when failures are rare or high-dimensional. Real trajectory spaces can have hundreds of dimensions, and failure distributions may be multi-modal.

A single Gaussian can therefore poorly approximate $q^*$ when the failure distribution has separated modes or strong non-Gaussian structure.

This motivates adaptive methods that learn proposal distributions from easier samples.

### The Cross-Entropy Method

The cross-entropy method (CEM) fits a new proposal distribution by minimizing cross-entropy to the failure distribution. Instead of directly sampling from the failure distribution, it draws samples from an easier proposal and uses importance weights to fit the next proposal.

For Gaussian proposals, this becomes a weighted maximum-likelihood update. Non-failure samples receive zero weight, while failure samples receive their usual importance weights:

$$
\hat{\mu} = \frac{\sum_i \tilde{w}_i \tau_i}{\sum_i \tilde{w}_i}, \quad \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\sum_i \tilde{w}_i (\tau_i - \hat{\mu})^2}{\sum_i \tilde{w}_i}
$$

$$
\tilde{w}_i = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \tau_i \text{ not a failure} \\ \dfrac{p(\tau_i)}{q(\tau_i)} & \text{if } \tau_i \text{ is a failure} \end{cases}
$$

The basic procedure is:

1. Sample trajectories from an initial easy proposal, often the nominal model.
2. Compute weights.
3. Fit a parametric proposal using weighted maximum likelihood.
4. Run importance sampling with the fitted proposal.

This works well when the initial proposal produces enough failure samples. It breaks down when failures are so rare that no sampled trajectory fails. In that case, all fitting weights are zero and the method has no information from which to update the proposal.

### Adaptive Cross-Entropy

Adaptive CEM solves the zero-failure problem by fitting to near-failures before fitting to true failures. The key ingredient is a robustness or closeness-to-failure function $f(\tau)$ satisfying:

$$
f(\tau) \leq 0 \iff \tau \text{ is a failure}
$$

Rather than immediately targeting $f(\tau) \leq 0$, adaptive CEM targets relaxed events:

$$
p(\tau \mid f(\tau) \leq \gamma), \quad \gamma = f(\tau_{(m_{\text{elite}})})
$$

Here, samples are ranked by $f(\tau)$, and the elite threshold $\gamma$ is set by the worst sample among the top $m_{\text{elite}}$ closest-to-failure trajectories. This guarantees that each iteration has at least some nonzero fitting weights.

Each iteration fits the next proposal by weighted MLE on elite samples from the current proposal. In implementation, the threshold is commonly capped at the actual failure boundary so the relaxation moves toward, but not past, the real event definition.

<details><summary>A compact version of the adaptive CEM loop is:</summary>

```julia
initialize q
for t = 1 to T:
    samples = draw from q
    compute f(tau) for each
    elite = top m_elite samples by smallest f
    gamma = max(f(worst_elite), 0)
    q = weighted_MLE(elite, weights = p/q * 1[f <= gamma])
final_estimate = IS with q
```

</details>

Adaptive CEM moves the proposal gradually from the nominal distribution toward the failure region. It is still stochastic and heuristic. A poor robustness function, unlucky samples, or a single Gaussian proposal in a multi-modal failure landscape can still lead to weak performance. For separated failure modes, Gaussian mixtures are often more appropriate than a single Gaussian.

### Multiple Importance Sampling

Many systems have multiple distinct ways to fail. A single proposal distribution may have to spread mass across several separated regions, which can increase variance. Multiple importance sampling (MIS) addresses this by combining samples from several proposals.

The general MIS estimator has the same shape as ordinary importance sampling:

$$
\hat{p}_{\text{fail}}^{\text{MIS}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \mathbb{1}[\tau_i \text{ failure}]
$$

The difference lies in how the weight $w_i$ is computed.

In standard MIS, each sample is weighted by the proposal that generated it:

$$
w_i^{\text{SMIS}} = \frac{p(\tau_i)}{q_{j(i)}(\tau_i)}
$$

where $q_{j(i)}$ is the source proposal for sample $\tau_i$.

An alternative is deterministic mixture MIS. Treat the proposals as components of a mixture:

$$
q_{\text{mix}}(\tau) = \sum_{j=1}^{K} \alpha_j \, q_j(\tau), \quad \sum_j \alpha_j = 1
$$

Then weight every sample by the mixture density:

$$
w_i^{\text{mix}} = \frac{p(\tau_i)}{q_{\text{mix}}(\tau_i)} = \frac{p(\tau_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \alpha_j \, q_j(\tau_i)}
$$

This can be counterintuitive because the physical sampling process may have drawn fixed batches from each proposal. The mixture-weighted estimator interprets those samples as though they came from a mixture distribution. Under the right assumptions, both approaches are valid, and mixture weighting often has lower variance because it accounts for all proposals that could have produced a sample.

### Population Monte Carlo

MIS is useful when good proposals are available, but hand-designing many proposals is hard. Population Monte Carlo (PMC) automates proposal discovery by evolving a population of proposal distributions.

PMC maintains proposals $\{q_1, \ldots, q_K\}$. At each iteration, it draws one sample from each proposal, assigns failure-weighted importance weights, resamples high-weight particles, and creates new proposals centered on the resampled points.

The particle weight is:

$$
w_i = \frac{p(\tau_i)}{q_i(\tau_i)} \cdot \mathbb{1}[\tau_i \text{ is a failure}]
$$

Particles are then resampled in proportion to these weights, and each resampled point becomes the center of a new local proposal. After $T$ iterations, the final proposal population is used for MIS.

<details><summary>The core loop is:</summary>

```julia
initialize population {q_1, ..., q_K} spread over space
for t = 1 to T:
    for i = 1 to K:
        tau_i ~ q_i
        w_i = (p(tau_i)/q_i(tau_i)) * 1[failure]
    resampled = multinomial_resample({tau_i}, {w_i})
    for i = 1 to K:
        q_i = Gaussian(mean=resampled[i], cov=Sigma_fixed)
estimate p_fail via MIS with final {q_i}
```

</details>

PMC is especially useful for multi-modal failure distributions because different proposals can migrate toward different failure modes. Its main weakness is the initial coverage requirement. If the initial population never samples a failure, all weights are zero and resampling cannot move the population usefully. The fixed covariance $\Sigma_{\text{fixed}}$ also matters: too small and the population collapses; too large and it fails to concentrate.

### Sequential Monte Carlo

Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) offers a different route. Instead of fitting explicit Gaussian proposals, it moves a particle population through a sequence of intermediate distributions that bridge the nominal distribution and the failure distribution.

The hard failure indicator can be replaced by a soft approximation:

$$
\tilde{\mathbb{1}}_\epsilon(f(\tau)) = \mathcal{N}(f(\tau) \mid 0, \epsilon) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{f(\tau)^2}{2\epsilon}\right)
$$

This gives intermediate unnormalized distributions:

$$
g_k(\tau) = p(\tau) \cdot \mathcal{N}(f(\tau) \mid 0, \epsilon_k)
$$

As $\epsilon_k$ decreases, the distribution concentrates closer to the failure boundary. Starting from nominal-like particles, SMC gradually migrates particles toward the failure distribution using MCMC moves, resampling, and sequential weight updates.

The weight update is:

$$
w_i^{(k)} = w_i^{(k-1)} \cdot \frac{g_k(\tau_i^{(k)})}{g_{k-1}(\tau_i^{(k-1)})}
$$

An alternative bridge uses thresholds rather than smoothing:

$$
g_k(\tau) \propto p(\tau) \cdot \mathbb{1}[f(\tau) \leq \gamma_k], \quad \gamma_1 \gg \cdots \gg \gamma_n = 0
$$

<details><summary>The SMC loop can be summarized as:</summary>

```julia
initialize particles from nominal p(tau)
w_i = 1 for all i
for k = 2 to n:
    optionally resample particles by w_i
    for each particle i:
        run MCMC targeting g_k, starting from tau_i
        tau_i = final MCMC state
        w_i *= g_k(tau_i) / g_{k-1}(tau_i_prev)
p_fail_hat = mean(w)
```

</details>

SMC is attractive for high-dimensional and multi-modal problems because it avoids fitting a simple parametric proposal to a complex distribution. Its cost is algorithmic complexity. The smoothing schedule $\epsilon_1 > \epsilon_2 > \cdots$ or threshold schedule $\gamma_1 > \gamma_2 > \cdots$ must be chosen carefully, and each stage may require MCMC moves for every particle.

### Failure Probability as a Normalizing Constant

There is a broader statistical view behind all of these methods. The failure probability is the normalizing constant of the unnormalized failure density:

$$
p_{\text{fail}} = Z_{\text{fail}} = \int p(\tau)\,\mathbb{1}[\text{failure}]\, d\tau
$$

More generally, many Monte Carlo methods estimate ratios of normalizing constants:

$$
\frac{Z_1}{Z_2} = \frac{\int \tilde{p}_1(\tau)\, d\tau}{\int \tilde{p}_2(\tau)\, d\tau}
$$

For failure probability estimation, $Z_1$ is the failure normalizer and $Z_2$ is the nominal normalizer, often equal to $1$. From this perspective, standard importance sampling, self-normalized importance sampling, bridge sampling, umbrella sampling, PMC, and SMC are different tools for estimating related normalizing-constant ratios.

The practical message is that rare-event estimation is not only a simulation problem. It is also a problem of estimating the mass of a difficult distribution.

### Practical Takeaways

Importance sampling is powerful because it separates where samples come from from what probability they estimate. By sampling from a proposal distribution and reweighting by $p/q$, it can turn impossible rare-event estimation problems into feasible computations.

The hard part is proposal design:

- Direct Monte Carlo is simple but inefficient for rare failures.
- A valid proposal must cover every possible failure region with nonzero nominal probability.
- The zero-variance proposal is the failure distribution itself, but it requires knowing $p_{\text{fail}}$.
- Cross-entropy methods fit tractable proposals to failure or near-failure samples.
- Adaptive CEM uses relaxed thresholds to move gradually toward rare failures.
- MIS combines multiple proposals and is useful for multi-modal failures.
- PMC automatically evolves a population of proposals but depends on initial coverage.
- SMC avoids parametric fitting by moving particles through intermediate distributions, at the cost of MCMC and schedule design.

All of these methods aim at the same target: approximate the failure distribution closely enough that failures are sampled often, while keeping likelihood weights stable enough to estimate the true nominal failure probability.

## 2. Reachability Analysis for Linear Systems: From Rollouts to Safety Guarantees

Simulation can find failures, estimate failure probabilities, and guide debugging, but it cannot prove safety from the absence of observed failures. Reachability analysis takes a different approach. Instead of sampling a finite number of trajectories, it computes the set of all states a system can reach under bounded assumptions about initial states, disturbances, and model behavior.

This explains reachability for linear systems using a mass-spring-damper system as the running example. The main idea is to replace pointwise simulation with set propagation: if the current state belongs to a set and the disturbances belong to bounded sets, then the next state belongs to a set obtained by linear transformations and Minkowski sums. With the right set representations, this gives formal finite-horizon safety guarantees, and sometimes even infinite-horizon guarantees.

### Why Reachability Is Different from Rollouts

Suppose a controlled mass-spring-damper system moves along one axis. The mass can be compressed too close to the wall or extended too far from its resting position. Those regions are failures. A controller applies force to reduce oscillation, but it only sees noisy observations of position and velocity.

Sampling-based analysis would draw initial states and disturbances, simulate the system, and check whether any rollout enters the failure region. If no rollout fails, we have learned something useful, but not enough to certify safety. A finite sample set can always miss a rare trajectory.

Reachability asks a stronger question: given a bounded initial set and bounded disturbances, what is the complete set of states the system could occupy at each timestep? Safety is then a set-intersection problem:

$$
\text{Given } \mathcal{X}_{\text{sensor}} \subset \mathbb{R},\; \exists\; \text{proof of safety?} \iff \mathcal{R} \cap \mathcal{A} = \emptyset
$$

Here $\mathcal{R}$ is the reachable set and $\mathcal{A}$ is the avoid set, or failure region. If the reachable set never intersects the avoid set, every trajectory satisfying the assumptions is safe.

For the mass-spring-damper example, the bounded initial set allows position $p$ to start in $[-0.2,0.2]$ with zero velocity. The sensor disturbance bounds both position and velocity error by one unit. Agent and environment disturbances are zero sets, while the avoid set contains states whose position magnitude exceeds $0.3$.

The important point is that samples are only a finite subset of the reachable set. They can illustrate system behavior, but they do not define the safety guarantee.

### The Formal Reachability Problem

Let $\mathcal{S}_0$ be the bounded initial set and let $\mathcal{X}$ be the bounded disturbance set. A reach function $\text{reach}(s, \mathbf{x}, d)$ takes an initial state $s$, a disturbance sequence $\mathbf{x}$ of length $d$, and returns the final state after $d$ steps.

The reachable set at exactly depth $d$ is:

```math
\mathcal{R}_d = \left\{ s' \;\middle|\; \exists\, s \in \mathcal{S}_0,\; \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}^d : \text{reach}(s, \mathbf{x}, d) = s' \right\}
```

For safety over a horizon, we usually need the union of all per-depth reachable sets:

$$
\mathcal{R}_{1:h} = \bigcup_{d=1}^{h} \mathcal{R}_d
$$

The finite-horizon safety condition is:

$$
\mathcal{R}_{1:h} \cap \mathcal{A} = \emptyset \implies \text{system safe on timesteps } 1, \ldots, h
$$

This definition also clarifies the modeling shift from probabilistic analysis. Previous failure-analysis methods might describe uncertainty with distributions. Reachability instead needs bounded sets. If a distribution has bounded support, the set can be its support:

$$
\mathcal{S} = \text{supp}(p) = \{x : p(x) > 0\}
$$

For Gaussian uncertainty, the support is infinite, so a bounded reachability problem requires an explicit truncation such as a three-, four-, or five-sigma interval. That truncation is not a mathematical identity. It is an explicit assumption, and the resulting guarantee is conditional on it.

### Linear System Structure

This focuses on linear systems because their reachable sets can be propagated in closed form through set operations. The observation model is linear:

$$
o = O_s s + O_x x_o
$$

The agent, or policy, is linear:

$$
a = \Pi_o o + \Pi_x x_a
$$

The transition model is linear:

$$
s' = T_s s + T_a a + T_x x_e
$$

The variables are:

- $s$: true system state
- $o$: observation available to the controller
- $a$: action or control input
- $x_o$: observation disturbance
- $x_a$: agent disturbance
- $x_e$: environment disturbance
- $O_s, O_x, \Pi_o, \Pi_x, T_s, T_a, T_x$: fixed matrices

Substituting the observation model into the policy, and the policy into the transition, lets the one-step dynamics be written compactly as:

$$
s' = A s + B x
$$

where $A = T_s + T_a \Pi_o O_s$ and $B$ collects disturbance columns from $T_a \Pi_o O_x$, $T_a \Pi_x$, $T_x$.

This affine form is the core reason linear reachability is tractable. A pointwise update uses matrix multiplication and vector addition. A set-valued update uses linear transformations of sets and Minkowski sums.

### Set Operations for Propagation

Two set operations drive the entire method.

The first is linear transformation:

$$
A\mathcal{P} = \{Ap : p \in \mathcal{P}\}
$$

This applies matrix $A$ to every point in the set $\mathcal{P}$.

The second is Minkowski sum:

$$
\mathcal{P} \oplus \mathcal{Q} = \{p + q : p \in \mathcal{P},\; q \in \mathcal{Q}\}
$$

This forms every pairwise vector sum between points in $\mathcal{P}$ and points in $\mathcal{Q}$. In reachability, Minkowski sums represent the way state uncertainty and disturbance uncertainty combine.

The set-valued version of the affine update is:

$$
\mathcal{P}' = A \mathcal{P} \oplus B \mathcal{X}
$$

For the full observation-agent-transition system, the one-step propagation equation expands to:

$$
\mathcal{R}_d = T_s \mathcal{R}_{d-1} \oplus T_a \Pi_o O_s \mathcal{R}_{d-1} \oplus T_a \Pi_o O_x \mathcal{X}_o \oplus T_a \Pi_x \mathcal{X}_a \oplus T_x \mathcal{X}_e
$$

The multi-step recurrence is:

$$
\mathcal{R}_1 = \mathcal{S}_0, \qquad \mathcal{R}_d = \text{Propagate}(\mathcal{R}_{d-1}, \mathcal{X}), \quad d = 2, \ldots, h
$$

<details><summary>In Julia with LazySets.jl, the mathematical structure maps directly to code:</summary>

```julia
# get_matrices(system) returns Ts, Ta, Pio, Os, Ox, Pix, Tx
function propagate(current_set, disturbance_sets)
    Ts, Ta, Pio, Os, Ox, Pix, Tx = get_matrices(system)
    Xo, Xa, Xe = disturbance_sets
    return Ts * current_set ⊕ Ta * Pio * Os * current_set ⊕ Ta * Pio * Ox * Xo ⊕ Ta * Pix * Xa ⊕ Tx * Xe
end
```

</details>

The operators are not symbolic decoration. `*` represents a linear transformation of a set, and `⊕` represents a Minkowski sum. Iterating this function constructs the reachable sets at successive depths.

### What Reachability Certifies

If the reachable set over the horizon avoids the failure set, safety is proved for that horizon:

$$
\mathcal{R}_{1:h} \cap \mathcal{A} = \emptyset \implies \forall t \in \{1,\ldots,h\}: s_t \notin \mathcal{A}
$$

If exact reachability shows intersection with the avoid set, then some valid trajectory enters the failure region.

This is where reachability differs sharply from sampling. It is possible for every sampled rollout to avoid failure while the exact reachable set intersects the avoid set.

In the mass-spring-damper demonstration, low sensor noise produces reachable sets that remain outside the red failure regions through the displayed horizon. Increasing the sensor noise expands the reachable sets until they overlap the avoid set. Even if rollout samples do not visibly fail, the reachable set certifies that a failure trajectory exists under the bounded assumptions.

These guarantees are finite-horizon unless additional structure appears. To prove safety forever, one useful condition is an invariant set:

$$
\mathcal{R}_d \subseteq \mathcal{R}_{d-1} \implies \mathcal{R}_d \text{ is forward-invariant} \implies \text{safe for all } t \geq d
$$

This also considers the relaxed condition that the current reachable set is contained in the union of previous reachable sets. This can be harder to check because the accumulated reachable set is usually a non-convex union. The simpler condition $R_d \subseteq R_{d-1}$ is easier to reason about, but it may not hold for every system.

All of these guarantees are conditional. They depend on the assumed initial set, disturbance bounds, model accuracy, and any truncation used to convert distributions into bounded sets.

### Choosing Useful Set Representations

The definitions above are mathematically clear but computationally useless if the sets are arbitrary point collections. Efficient reachability needs set representations with three properties:

- Finite representation: infinitely many states can be described compactly.
- Efficient operations: linear transforms, Minkowski sums, intersections, and containment checks can be computed without enumerating all points.
- Closure under operations: applying an operation to a set type preferably returns the same set type.

Convexity is the starting point: if two points lie in the set, then the line segment between them also lies in the set.

A polytope is a bounded intersection of finitely many half-spaces. In H-representation, it is described by linear inequalities:

$$
\mathcal{P} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : Ax \leq b\}
$$

In V-representation, it is described as the convex hull of vertices:

$$
\mathcal{P} = \text{conv}\{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_m\}
$$

Both representations describe the same class of bounded convex sets, but they are convenient for different operations. V-polytopes are especially natural for the two operations used in propagation. A linear transform becomes:

$$
A\mathcal{P} = \text{conv}\{Av_1, Av_2, \ldots, Av_m\}
$$

The Minkowski sum of two V-polytopes can be computed from all pairwise vertex sums:

$$
\mathcal{P} \oplus \mathcal{Q} = \text{conv}\{v_i + w_j : v_i \in \mathcal{P},\; w_j \in \mathcal{Q}\}
$$

This is exact, but it points to the next problem: the number of candidate vertices grows quickly.

### Vertex Growth and Zonotopes

V-polytopes preserve exactness, but repeated Minkowski sums can cause exponential vertex growth. Each step combines vertices from the previous reachable set with vertices from each disturbance set, so the candidate vertex count can multiply across the horizon.

This introduces zonotopes as a tractable alternative. A zonotope is a symmetric polytope represented by a center $c$ and generators $g_1,\ldots,g_p$:

```math
\mathcal{Z}(c, G) = \left\{c + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \alpha_i g_i : \alpha_i \in [-1, 1]\right\}
```

Geometrically, it is the Minkowski sum of line segments centered at $c$. Every zonotope is a polytope, but not every polytope is a zonotope because zonotopes are centrally symmetric.

The advantage is computational. Linear transforms apply to the center and generators:

$$
A\mathcal{Z}(c, G) = \mathcal{Z}(Ac, \{Ag_i\}_{i=1}^p)
$$

Minkowski sums add centers and concatenate generator lists:

$$
\mathcal{Z}(c_1, G_1) \oplus \mathcal{Z}(c_2, G_2) = \mathcal{Z}(c_1 + c_2,\; G_1 \cup G_2)
$$

So the generator count grows linearly under repeated sums instead of multiplying as vertex counts do. Hyperrectangles are a special case of zonotopes with axis-aligned generators. The hierarchy is:

$$
\text{polytopes} \supset \text{zonotopes} \supset \text{hyperrectangles}
$$

The tradeoff is expressiveness. Zonotopes are efficient, but they cannot represent every reachable polytope exactly.

### Overapproximation

When exact propagation is too expensive, reachability can use an overapproximation:

$$
\mathcal{R} \subseteq \bar{\mathcal{R}}
$$

The bar indicates that $\bar{\mathcal{R}}$ contains the true reachable set. This changes the interpretation of safety checks. If $\bar{\mathcal{R}} \cap \mathcal{A} = \emptyset$, the system is safe under the model assumptions because the true reachable set is contained inside the approximation. If $\bar{\mathcal{R}} \cap \mathcal{A} \neq \emptyset$, the result is inconclusive because the approximation may include unreachable states.

This asymmetry is central. Overapproximation preserves the ability to prove safety, but it can produce false alarms or inconclusive intersections.

An implementation may periodically replace the current set with a simpler set that contains it, using a chosen error or complexity budget. The parameter $\varepsilon$ controls the approximation error. This gives a practical route through the scalability problem: exact propagation where possible, zonotopes where their structure is appropriate, and controlled overapproximation when set complexity becomes too large.

### Practical Takeaways

Reachability analysis changes the question from "did any sampled rollout fail?" to "can any trajectory satisfying the assumptions enter the avoid set?" For linear systems, the answer can be computed by repeatedly applying linear set transforms and Minkowski sums.

The main ideas are:

- Reachable sets contain all states possible under bounded initial states and bounded disturbances.
- Safety over a horizon is certified by checking $\mathcal{R}_{1:h} \cap \mathcal{A} = \emptyset$.
- Linear observation, policy, and transition models reduce to an affine update $s' = A s + B x$.
- Set propagation replaces matrix-vector multiplication with linear set transformation, and vector addition with Minkowski sum.
- Exact polytope propagation can suffer exponential vertex growth.
- Zonotopes trade expressiveness for tractable generator-based propagation.
- Overapproximation keeps computations manageable while preserving sound safety proofs.
- Every guarantee is only as valid as its assumptions: bounded initial set, bounded disturbances, known model, and any distribution truncation.

## 3. From Failure Sampling to Failure Probability Estimation

Safety analysis often begins with a simple question: can the system fail? Once failures can be found, the next question is harder and more useful: how likely are those failures under the nominal operating distribution? This shift moves the problem from falsification to sampling and then to probability estimation.

This explains that progression. It starts with Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling of failure distributions, shows why smoothing can make finite-sample MCMC more practical, then formalizes the probability of failure and compares direct Monte Carlo estimation with importance sampling. The central idea is that rare failures are difficult because the nominal system almost never generates the trajectories we care about, so we need sampling methods that explore failure regions without corrupting the final probability estimate.

### Sampling from a Failure Distribution

A trajectory $\tau$ represents one complete rollout of a stochastic system. The failure set $\mathcal{F}$ contains all trajectories that violate the desired safety property. The failure distribution is the nominal trajectory distribution restricted to this failure set and normalized.

In principle, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) can sample from an unnormalized failure density. In the infinite-sample limit, the chain should explore the target distribution correctly. In practice, the sample budget is finite, and the chain can get stuck.

The problem is especially clear for multi-modal failure spaces. Suppose there are two separated ways to fail. A naive random-walk MCMC kernel may explore whichever failure mode it starts in, but it may almost never propose a large enough jump to reach the other mode. The chain is theoretically correct in the limit, but the finite sample set can badly misrepresent the true failure distribution.

Standard MCMC practices help, but they do not solve the whole problem. A burn-in period discards early samples before the chain has reached a representative region. Thinning keeps every few samples to reduce correlation. These techniques improve sample quality, but they do not force a chain to cross low-probability gaps between separated failure modes.

### Smoothing the Failure Indicator

Smoothing addresses this exploration problem by relaxing the hard boundary of the failure set. Instead of assigning probability only to failures, it assigns some probability to near-failure trajectories. This gives MCMC a bridge between separated failure modes.

The first step is to define a distance-to-failure function:

$$
\delta(\tau) = \max(\text{robustness}(\tau), 0)
$$

Here, $\delta(\tau)=0$ when $\tau$ is a failure, and $\delta(\tau)>0$ when the trajectory is safe. The robustness value is positive for non-failures, so taking the maximum with zero creates a nonnegative distance measure.

The hard failure indicator can then be approximated by a narrow Gaussian centered at zero:

$$
\mathbb{I}(\tau \in \mathcal{F}) \approx \mathcal{N}(\delta(\tau) \mid 0, \epsilon)
$$

The parameter $\epsilon$ controls the amount of smoothing. With very small $\epsilon$, the smoothed density remains close to the original failure density. With larger $\epsilon$, the density gives more weight to near-failures, which makes it easier for MCMC to move through the surrounding trajectory space.

This creates a practical trade-off:

- Smaller $\epsilon$ keeps samples closer to true failures but may not help the chain move between modes.
- Larger $\epsilon$ improves exploration but produces more non-failure samples.
- After sampling from the smoothed distribution, non-failure samples must be rejected to recover samples from the actual failure distribution.

This final rejection step is important. Smoothing is not changing the target engineering question; it is a computational device that helps the sampler explore. Once the chain has used the smoothed density to move around, the final retained set should still contain only true failures.

### Why Smoothing Helps in Practice

In a bimodal failure distribution, a lightly smoothed density may still leave a large low-probability gap between the modes. The chain may continue to remain in one mode for the duration of a finite run. Increasing $\epsilon$ widens the smoothed region and can allow the chain to jump back and forth more often.

The cost is wasted samples. Some trajectories sampled under the smoothed density are near failures but not actual failures, so they are discarded after rejection. This is still worthwhile if the smoothing enables the chain to discover all relevant failure modes.

The same idea scales beyond one-dimensional examples. In a high-dimensional system such as a pendulum, a sample is not a single scalar. It includes the initial state and the full sequence of disturbances that define an entire trajectory. MCMC proposes whole trajectories, accepts or rejects them according to the target density, and can use smoothing to explore high-dimensional failure regions more effectively.

Choosing $\epsilon$ remains a modeling and diagnostic problem. Too little smoothing leaves the chain trapped. Too much smoothing creates many non-failure samples and can reduce efficiency. Domain knowledge, long baseline runs, and inspection of discovered failure modes can help determine whether the sampler is plausibly exploring the full failure space.

### More Efficient MCMC and Probabilistic Programming

Naive MCMC often behaves like a random walk. More advanced methods use gradients of the target log-density to move more intelligently through high-likelihood regions. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) are standard examples. These methods can be much more efficient in high-dimensional spaces because they use geometric information rather than proposing purely local random perturbations.

Probabilistic programming offers another practical abstraction. Instead of manually implementing every inference step, the analyst specifies the probabilistic rollout model and the desired target density. The program samples an initial state and disturbances, rolls out the system, and scores the trajectory under the smoothed failure density. The inference engine then runs an MCMC method such as HMC or NUTS.

### Defining the Probability of Failure

Sampling failures is useful, but safety analysis often needs a probability. The probability of failure is the expected value of the failure indicator under the nominal trajectory distribution:

$$
p_{\text{fail}} = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim P}[\mathbb{I}(\tau \in \mathcal{F})] = \int \mathbb{I}(\tau \in \mathcal{F}) P(\tau) d\tau
$$

This equation says that failure probability is the total nominal probability mass assigned to failing trajectories. It is also the normalizing constant of the failure distribution. That connection explains why estimating $p_{\text{fail}}$ is difficult: the same denominator that made the failure distribution hard to normalize is exactly the quantity we now want to compute.

### Direct Estimation

The most straightforward estimator is direct Monte Carlo. Simulate $m$ trajectories from the nominal system and count how many fail:

$$
\hat{p}_{\text{fail}} = \frac{N_{\text{fail}}}{m}
$$

This estimator is intuitive, unbiased, and consistent. If enough samples are drawn, it converges to the true failure probability.

Its weakness is rare events. If $p_{\text{fail}}$ is extremely small, a finite simulation campaign may observe no failures at all. The maximum likelihood estimate then becomes $\hat{p}_{\text{fail}}=0$, which is usually not a defensible safety conclusion. It means the test did not observe a failure, not that the system cannot fail.

Although the estimator variance decreases as $m$ grows, the number of samples required for useful relative accuracy can be enormous when failures are rare.

A Bayesian treatment gives a more cautious interpretation. Instead of returning only the fraction of observed failures, it treats $p_{\text{fail}}$ as an unknown parameter with a posterior distribution. Even if zero failures are observed, the posterior can still support statements such as a confidence or credible upper bound on the failure rate. This is more useful than declaring the failure probability to be exactly zero.

### Importance Sampling

Importance sampling improves rare-event estimation by sampling from a proposal distribution $Q$ instead of the nominal distribution $P$. The proposal is chosen to generate failures more often. Because this changes the sampling distribution, each sample must be corrected by an importance weight.

The change-of-measure identity rewrites the nominal expectation as an expectation under $Q$, corrected by the likelihood ratio $P(\tau)/Q(\tau)$.

The corresponding estimator is:

$$
\hat{p}_{\text{fail}} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m W_i \mathbb{I}(\tau_i \in \mathcal{F}), \quad \text{where } W_i = \frac{P(\tau_i)}{Q(\tau_i)}
$$

The proposal $Q$ deliberately biases the samples toward failure. The weight $W_i$ corrects that bias by comparing how likely the sampled trajectory is under the nominal distribution versus the proposal distribution.

This means importance sampling requires more than the ability to draw samples from $Q$. The analyst must also be able to evaluate both $P(\tau_i)$ and $Q(\tau_i)$, at least up to the normalized densities needed for the ratio. If the proposal can generate samples but cannot score their likelihood, it cannot be used directly for standard importance sampling.

Operationally, each trajectory is sampled from $Q$, checked for failure, weighted by $P/Q$, and accumulated into an average. The formula above is the complete estimator; the implementation detail is mainly bookkeeping around sampling, failure checking, and density evaluation.

### The Optimal Proposal

The ideal proposal distribution for importance sampling is the true failure distribution: the nominal distribution restricted to failing trajectories and normalized. If $Q=Q^*$, every sample is a failure. For every sampled trajectory, the importance weight becomes exactly $p_{\text{fail}}$. The estimator then has zero variance.

This result is mathematically elegant but practically unavailable. Defining $Q^*$ requires knowing $p_{\text{fail}}$, which is the unknown quantity we are trying to estimate. The optimal proposal therefore serves as a target for approximation rather than a directly usable distribution.

### Building Practical Proposals

One practical strategy is to first sample from the failure distribution using MCMC, possibly with smoothing, and then fit a tractable parametric distribution to those samples. For example, one can fit a Gaussian to a collection of failure trajectories. The fitted Gaussian becomes the proposal $Q$ for importance sampling.

This bridges the earlier sampling problem and the later estimation problem:

1. Use MCMC to obtain representative failure samples from an unnormalized or smoothed failure density.
2. Fit a known, normalized proposal distribution to those samples.
3. Use that proposal in importance sampling, where $Q(\tau)$ can be evaluated.
4. Estimate $p_{\text{fail}}$ with weighted failure samples.

The approach is an engineering heuristic, but a powerful one. MCMC can discover where failures live, while the fitted proposal provides the normalized density needed for importance weights.

The approximation quality matters. A single Gaussian may work for a compact, roughly elliptical failure region. It may fail for separated modes or strongly non-Gaussian trajectory distributions. In those cases, mixtures or adaptive proposal-learning methods may be necessary.

### Practical Takeaways

The methods form a sequence of increasingly capable tools:

- Falsification asks whether any failure can be found.
- Failure-distribution sampling asks what failures look like and how they are distributed.
- Direct estimation asks how often failures occur under the nominal distribution.
- Importance sampling asks a better sampling distribution to produce failures more often, then corrects the bias with likelihood ratios.

The key technical ideas are:

- MCMC can sample from unnormalized failure densities, but finite chains may miss failure modes.
- Smoothing replaces a hard indicator with a soft density around the failure boundary.
- Non-failure samples from a smoothed distribution must be rejected when the goal is the true failure distribution.
- The failure probability is the normalizing constant of the failure distribution.
- Direct Monte Carlo is unbiased but inefficient for rare failures.
- Importance sampling can dramatically reduce variance, provided the proposal covers the relevant failure region and has an evaluable density.
- The zero-variance proposal is the true failure distribution, but it is unavailable because it depends on $p_{\text{fail}}$.

## 4. Nonlinear Reachability with Support Vectors, Intervals, and Taylor Inclusion Functions

Reachability analysis asks which states a dynamical system can reach from a set of initial conditions under allowed disturbances. For safety analysis, this usually means computing a reachable set and checking whether it intersects an avoid set. If the computed set is exact, the answer is definitive. If the computed set is an overapproximation, the logic is still useful: when the overapproximation avoids the unsafe region, the true reachable set is safe; when it intersects the unsafe region, the result is inconclusive rather than unsafe.

The central difficulty is that exact reachable sets quickly become too complex to manipulate. Linear systems already suffer from combinatorial growth in polytope vertices. Nonlinear systems are harder still because nonlinear transformations can bend simple convex sets into curved, non-convex shapes. This walks through three tools for making the problem computationally manageable: support-vector overapproximations, interval arithmetic, and Taylor inclusion functions.

### Why Overapproximation Is Necessary

For linear dynamics, propagating a polytope exactly through time can cause the number of vertices to grow exponentially. Zonotopes help for some systems, but they cannot represent every reachable-set geometry. The general strategy is therefore to periodically replace a complicated exact set with a simpler set that safely contains it.

This replacement introduces conservativeness. A larger set may touch an avoid region even if the true reachable set does not. But the benefit is tractability: the analysis can continue without explicitly carrying every vertex and every geometric detail of the exact reachable set.

The guiding principle is simple:

- If the overapproximation is disjoint from the avoid set, the true system is safe over that horizon.
- If the overapproximation intersects the avoid set, the analysis cannot conclude safety from that bound alone.
- Tighter overapproximations reduce false alarms, but they usually cost more computation.

### Bounding Reachable Sets with Support Vectors

A support vector describes the farthest point of a set in a chosen direction. For a set $\mathcal{S}$ and direction $d$, it is defined as:

$$
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}(d) = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{S}} (x \cdot d)
$$

Geometrically, this is the point where a hyperplane perpendicular to $d$ just touches the set after being pushed as far as possible in that direction. The corresponding maximum projection defines a bounding half-space. By repeating this calculation for multiple directions, we can construct a bounding polyhedron around the reachable set.

The choice of directions matters. Axis-aligned directions are simple, but they may produce a loose box if the reachable set is diagonal or elongated. Directions chosen from sampled rollouts can be much more informative. For example, applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to a small set of simulated trajectories can reveal the dominant directions in which the reachable set spreads, giving tighter bounds with fewer support-vector queries.

### Computing Future Support Vectors as Linear Programs

The useful part of support-vector reachability is that we do not necessarily need to first construct the exact reachable set and then bound it. For linear dynamics, we can solve directly for the state at a future time that maximizes projection in direction $d$.

The optimization problem has the form:

$$
\max s_d \cdot d \quad \text{subject to } s_1 \in \mathcal{S}_0, \ x_t \in \mathcal{X}_t, \ s_{t+1} = As_t + Bx_t
$$

Here, $s_d$ is the state at the target depth, $\mathcal{S}_0$ is the initial state set, $\mathcal{X}_t$ is the disturbance set at time $t$, and $A,B$ define the linear transition dynamics. The objective pushes the final state as far as possible in direction $d$, while the constraints ensure that the entire trajectory obeys the initial set, disturbance bounds, and system dynamics.

Because the dynamics are linear and the sets are typically represented with linear constraints, this becomes a linear program. Tools such as `JuMP.jl` can express the optimization, while set libraries such as `LazySets.jl` can represent the resulting overapproximations.

This elegant LP formulation depends on linearity. Once the dynamics become nonlinear, the same direct optimization generally becomes non-convex and much harder to solve globally.

### Why Nonlinear Systems Are Harder

Linear maps preserve polytopes: applying a linear function to a polytope yields another polytope. Nonlinear functions do not have that property. A nonlinear map such as $\sin(x)$ can transform a simple interval or polytope into a curved, non-convex image. In higher dimensions and across multiple time steps, those shapes can become mathematically awkward and computationally expensive to represent exactly.

This is why nonlinear reachability often gives up on exact shape propagation and instead uses set-based arithmetic that guarantees containment. The most basic version is interval arithmetic.

### Interval Arithmetic

An interval represents all scalar values between a lower and upper bound. If $X = [\underline{x}, \overline{x}]$, then $X$ contains every value $x$ such that $\underline{x} \leq x \leq \overline{x}$. In multiple dimensions, the Cartesian product of scalar intervals forms an interval box, or hyperrectangle.

Interval arithmetic defines counterparts for ordinary arithmetic operations. Instead of applying an operation to single values, it applies the operation to all possible values in the input intervals and returns an interval that contains every possible result.

For addition, the bounds combine directly: the smallest possible sum uses the two lower bounds, and the largest possible sum uses the two upper bounds. Subtraction crosses the bounds: the smallest possible value of $x-y$ uses the smallest $x$ and the largest $y$, while the largest possible value uses the largest $x$ and the smallest $y$.

For monotonic elementary functions, the interval image can often be found by evaluating the endpoints. For non-monotonic functions, the operation must account for extrema inside the interval. For example, if an interval contains zero, the minimum of $x^2$ over that interval is zero, even if neither endpoint is zero.

Libraries such as `IntervalArithmetic.jl` implement these rules so that ordinary-looking expressions can propagate rigorous interval bounds.

### The Cost of Interval Boxes

Interval arithmetic is rigorous, but it is geometrically limited. In multiple dimensions, an interval abstraction produces hyperrectangles. Hyperrectangles are easy to store and manipulate, but they ignore correlations between variables.

This causes overapproximation. A true reachable set may be thin, diagonal, curved, or otherwise strongly correlated across dimensions. An axis-aligned interval box must cover all of it, including empty regions that are not actually reachable. This geometric looseness is one source of conservativeness.

The second source is the dependency effect.

### Inclusion Functions and the Dependency Effect

An inclusion function `[f](X)` is an algorithm that returns an interval guaranteed to contain the true range of $f$ over $X$. The simplest construction is the natural inclusion function: replace each ordinary operation in $f$ with its interval counterpart.

This is easy and sound, but it can be extremely loose. The problem is that interval arithmetic treats repeated appearances of the same variable as if they were independent. For example, in an expression such as $x - \sin(x)$, the two occurrences of $x$ are physically tied to the same value, but interval arithmetic may allow them to vary independently inside the same interval. That artificial independence inflates the resulting bounds.

For deep nonlinear rollouts, the dependency effect can compound over time. Each step introduces new interval expressions that contain repeated variables and previously overapproximated sets. The reachable set can grow so quickly that safety verification becomes impossible even when the true system remains safe.

### Mean Value Inclusion Functions

Mean value inclusion functions reduce dependency by linearizing the function around a center point. The mathematical basis is the mean value theorem:

$$
f(x) - f(c) = f'(x')(x - c)
$$

Here, $c$ is the center of the interval and $x'$ is some point in the interval. The exact location of $x'$ is unknown, but its derivative can be bounded with interval arithmetic.

The corresponding mean value inclusion function is:

```math
[f]_{MV}(X) = f(c) + [\nabla f](X) \cdot (X - c)
```

This form has an important advantage. The term $f(c)$ is evaluated at a concrete scalar center, so it shows no interval width. Interval uncertainty appears mainly in the gradient bound `[∇ f](X)` and the centered displacement $(X-c)$. Because the displacement is centered around zero, the resulting interval is often much tighter than the natural inclusion function.

Conceptually, the method keeps the reliable part of the calculation exact and wraps only the nonlinear uncertainty in an interval bound.

### Taylor Inclusion Functions

The mean value inclusion function is a first-order Taylor-style method. The same idea extends to higher-order Taylor expansions: approximate the nonlinear function with a Taylor polynomial and bound the remainder term with interval arithmetic.

Higher-order Taylor inclusion functions can produce tighter bounds because they capture more of the local nonlinear structure before resorting to interval overapproximation. However, they do not eliminate the core challenge. Across many sequential time steps, nonlinearities stack recursively, and even Taylor-based interval bounds can eventually become too conservative.

The key implementation idea is that the gradient is evaluated over an interval input, producing an interval-valued derivative bound. Julia's multiple dispatch and automatic differentiation make this style natural: the same high-level function can often be evaluated on scalar values or interval values without manually rewriting every derivative rule.

### Practical Trade-Offs

These methods form a progression from simpler geometry to tighter nonlinear reasoning:

- Support-vector bounds are powerful for linear systems because each direction can be handled with a linear program.
- Direction choice is critical; PCA on sampled rollouts can guide the solver toward informative bounding directions.
- Interval arithmetic gives rigorous bounds for nonlinear expressions, but it returns axis-aligned boxes.
- Natural inclusion functions are easy to construct but can suffer severe dependency-effect inflation.
- Mean value and Taylor inclusion functions reduce dependency by evaluating the function around a center and bounding derivative or remainder terms.
- Higher-order Taylor methods improve local tightness, but long-horizon nonlinear reachability can still blow up from compounded overapproximation.

The core engineering tension is between soundness, tightness, and cost. A sound method must contain all possible states. A tight method avoids excessive false alarms. A practical method must run fast enough to be useful over the desired horizon.

## 5. From Taylor Models to Discrete and Probabilistic Reachability

Reachability analysis often moves between two different worlds. In continuous systems, the main challenge is geometry: nonlinear dynamics bend reachable sets into shapes that are hard to represent exactly. In discrete systems, the geometry disappears, but the analysis becomes a graph problem over states and transitions. The bridge between these worlds is abstraction: use sound continuous reachability tools to build a discrete graph, then reason about that graph efficiently.

This explains that progression. It starts with Taylor models and conservative linearization, which improve continuous nonlinear reachability beyond interval boxes. It then shows concrete reachability and the wrapping effect. Finally, it shows how discrete reachability, probabilistic reachability, and continuous-to-discrete abstraction turn safety questions into graph search and dynamic programming problems.

### Why Taylor Models Are Needed

Interval arithmetic and Taylor inclusion functions provide rigorous bounds for nonlinear systems, but they have a major geometric limitation: interval inputs produce interval outputs. In multiple dimensions, that means the reachable set is represented as a hyperrectangle. Hyperrectangles are simple and safe, but they can be extremely loose when the true reachable set is diagonal, curved, thin, or otherwise correlated across dimensions.

Taylor models address this limitation by separating the main functional approximation from the error bound. Instead of pushing only intervals through a nonlinear function, a Taylor model represents the function as a polynomial approximation plus an interval remainder. The polynomial can act on richer set representations, while the interval remainder bounds the truncation error.

Conceptually, the polynomial captures the shape of the nonlinear transformation, and the interval remainder guarantees soundness. This lets the analysis move beyond purely axis-aligned boxes while still preserving the conservative containment needed for safety verification.

### Conservative Linearization

A particularly useful Taylor model is conservative linearization. It is based on a first-order Taylor expansion with a rigorous error bound:

$$
f(x) \approx f(c) + J(x - c) + \alpha
$$

Here, $c$ is the center point, $J$ is the Jacobian matrix at that point, and $\alpha$ is an interval remainder that bounds the truncation error.

The practical value of this formula is that it turns a nonlinear reachability step into a linear set transformation plus bounded uncertainty. The term $J(x-c)$ is linear. If the input set is a polytope, multiplying it by $J$ produces another polytope. The offset $f(c)$ translates the set, and the interval remainder $\alpha$ is added through a Minkowski sum.

This is a major improvement over interval-only methods. Instead of forcing every intermediate result into a hyperrectangle, conservative linearization can propagate convex polytopes through a nonlinear system. The result remains an overapproximation, but it can be much tighter because the set geometry is allowed to rotate, stretch, and shear with the local linearization.

The limitation is also clear. If the function is highly nonlinear over the input set, the linear approximation may be poor and the interval remainder may become large. In that case, the resulting set can still blow up. Taylor models improve the representation, but they do not remove the fundamental trade-off between soundness, tightness, and computational cost.

### Symbolic Reachability Versus Concrete Reachability

Once a nonlinear system is propagated over multiple steps, the analysis must decide how much of the rollout to handle at once.

Symbolic reachability treats a multi-step rollout as one large composed function. For example, instead of explicitly constructing the reachable set at every intermediate time, it may analyze the map from the initial set directly to the state several steps later. This avoids repeatedly re-bounding intermediate sets, so it can reduce geometric wrapping error. The downside is that the composed function becomes increasingly complex. Gradients, Jacobians, and Taylor remainders are taken through many nested nonlinear steps, so nonlinearities compound.

Concrete reachability takes the opposite approach. It propagates the reachable set one step, constructs an explicit overapproximation of the next set, and then uses that concrete set as the input to the following step. This makes each local reachability calculation simpler because it only handles one step of dynamics at a time.

The cost is the wrapping effect. Every time the analysis concretizes an intermediate set, it replaces the true reachable set with a simpler overapproximation. That overapproximation includes states that are not actually reachable. On the next step, those fake states are treated as possible starting points, creating more fake volume. Repeating this process can make the reachable set grow far beyond the true system behavior.

Neither approach dominates universally:

- Symbolic reachability can avoid repeated wrapping, but it may suffer from expensive and loose multi-step nonlinear expansions.
- Concrete reachability is computationally local and often easier to implement, but it can accumulate overapproximation from repeated concretization.
- Hybrid strategies can concretize every few steps instead of every step or only at the final horizon.

### Partitioning as a Practical Remedy

One of the most effective ways to reduce Taylor-model error and wrapping error is partitioning. Instead of analyzing one large initial set, split it into many smaller subsets, run reachability on each subset, and union the results.

This works because nonlinear functions are easier to approximate locally. A linearization over a small region usually has a smaller remainder than a linearization over a large region. Similarly, bounding a small set usually introduces less fake volume than bounding a large set. The final reachable set may be non-convex, but it can be represented as a union of simpler convex overapproximations.

Partitioning trades computation for tightness. More partitions mean more reachability problems to solve, but each one is easier to approximate accurately. In many practical systems, that trade is worthwhile because it can turn an inconclusive overapproximation into a useful safety proof.

### Discrete Reachability as Graph Search

Discrete reachability removes the continuous geometry entirely. A discrete system has a finite or enumerable set of states. Each state becomes a node in a directed graph, and each possible transition becomes an edge.

For basic reachability, edge probabilities do not matter. The only question is whether a transition can occur. If the probability of moving from one state to another is nonzero, the graph contains an edge.

The forward reachable set from an initial state is computed by following outgoing edges. Repeating this process step by step is exactly breadth-first search. At depth one, collect all successors of the initial state. At depth two, collect successors of those states. Continue until the desired horizon is reached or no new states appear.

Backward reachability reverses the direction of the question. Starting from a target or avoid set, it follows incoming edges backward to find all states that could eventually lead to the target. This is especially useful for safety analysis: if the backward reachable set from an unsafe region intersects the initial set, then there exists a path from the initial condition to the unsafe region.

In implementation, graph construction amounts to enumerating states, computing each state's successors, and adding directed edges for all nonzero-probability transitions. For ordinary reachability, the weights can be ignored. For probabilistic reachability, they become essential.

### Invariant Sets and Safety Checks

Discrete systems make invariant-set detection straightforward. If a reachability computation reaches a depth where no new states are discovered, the reachable set has stabilized. If all states reachable through depth $d$ were already reachable before step $d$, the search has found a closed set. Continuing the traversal will not discover anything new.

This gives an infinite-time safety argument for finite graphs. If the stabilized reachable set does not intersect the avoid set, then the system can never reach the avoid set from the given initial conditions.

Safety checking can also be framed as satisfiability. The question is not always "what is the full reachable set?" Sometimes it is only "does there exist a path to failure?" If a single violating path is enough, heuristic search, backward search, or SAT-style methods may find a counterexample much faster than exhaustive reachable-set construction.

### Probabilistic Reachability

Discrete graphs can also answer probabilistic questions when the edges carry transition probabilities. Deterministic reachability asks whether a state is possible. Probabilistic reachability asks how likely it is.

There are two related but distinct quantities.

The first is probability of occupancy: the probability of being in a particular state at a particular time. It propagates probability mass forward through incoming edges:

$$
P(s, t+1) = \sum_{s'} P(s', t) \cdot P(s' \to s)
$$

This is the standard forward propagation equation for a Markov chain. The probability of occupying state $s$ at time $t+1$ is the sum of the probability of being in each predecessor state $s'$ at time $t$, multiplied by the transition probability from $s'$ to $s$. Because this describes where the system is at a specific time, the occupancy probabilities across all states sum to one.

The second quantity is the probability of reaching a target set within a time horizon. This is a different question: starting from state $s$, what is the chance of eventually hitting the target within $t+1$ steps? The recursion looks forward through outgoing edges:

$$
R_{t+1}(s) = \sum_{s'} R_t(s') \cdot P(s \to s')
$$

Here, $R_t(s')$ is the probability of reaching the target within $t$ steps starting from successor state $s'$. The current state's reachability probability is the expected value of its successors' reachability probabilities.

Unlike occupancy probabilities, these values do not need to sum to one across states. Many different starting states can each have a high probability of reaching the same target.

### From Continuous Systems to Discrete Abstractions

The most important connection is that discrete reachability can be used to reason about continuous systems through abstraction.

Start with a continuous state space and partition it into cells, such as grid boxes. Each cell becomes a discrete node. Then, for each cell, run a continuous reachability method to compute a conservative one-step reachable set. Every cell that intersects that reachable set becomes a successor node in the discrete graph.

This process converts continuous dynamics into a directed graph. The graph is conservative because the continuous reachable set is conservative: if the true system can move from one cell to another, the graph should include that transition. It may include extra transitions caused by overapproximation, but it should not omit real transitions.

That soundness property is what makes the abstraction useful. If the discrete graph is proven safe, then the original continuous system is safe under the assumptions of the reachability computation. If the graph reaches an unsafe node, the result may be a real counterexample or an artifact of overapproximation and discretization.

In this sense, abstraction trades one kind of difficulty for another:

- Continuous reachability struggles with nonlinear geometry and overapproximation.
- Discrete reachability avoids geometry but can suffer from the curse of dimensionality.
- Continuous-to-discrete abstraction combines them by using continuous reachability to construct a sound graph, then using graph algorithms to reason over long or infinite horizons.

### Practical Takeaways

The methods form a coherent pipeline:

- Taylor models improve nonlinear continuous reachability by allowing richer set geometries than interval boxes.
- Conservative linearization turns a nonlinear step into a linear set transformation plus a rigorous interval remainder.
- Concrete reachability handles one step at a time, reducing local nonlinear complexity but introducing wrapping error.
- Partitioning reduces overapproximation by analyzing smaller local regions and unioning the results.
- Discrete reachability represents states as graph nodes and transitions as directed edges.
- Forward and backward graph search compute possible behavior and unsafe predecessors.
- Probabilistic reachability uses transition weights to compute occupancy probabilities and target-hitting probabilities.
- Continuous abstraction turns continuous cells into graph nodes, enabling graph algorithms to verify continuous systems conservatively.

The recurring theme is controlled overapproximation. Each method accepts some conservativeness in exchange for tractability, while preserving the safety logic that makes reachability analysis useful.
